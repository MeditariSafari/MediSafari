October 14th, 2026The five-minute version

The readings: Jesus is still at the dinner table, and he isn't done. The Pharisees, he says, carefully give God a tenth of even their kitchen herbs, mint and rue, but neglect justice and the love of God. They love the best seats and being greeted in public. When a scholar of the law objects, Jesus turns to him: you load people with heavy burdens and won't lift a finger to help. In Galatians, Paul gives the alternative: a life led by the Spirit grows fruit, "love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, generosity, faithfulness, gentleness, self-control."

What it means: It's possible to be very careful about small rules and miss the big things entirely. Jesus doesn't say tithing is wrong; he says "these you should have done, without overlooking the others." But the heart of faith is justice and love. And a faith that piles burdens on other people without helping carry them isn't faith at all. Paul's list is a good mirror: not "What rules am I keeping?" but "Is love, patience and kindness growing in me?"

Across the traditions: the same truth, in other words.

  • Buddha: fine words without action are "like a beautiful flower, full of colour, but without scent."
  • Confucius: the superior man "acts before he speaks."
  • Marcus Aurelius: "No longer talk at all about the kind of man that a good man ought to be, but be such."
  • Tao Te Ching: "Sincere words are not fine; fine words are not sincere."
  • Kahlil Gibran: "Work is love made visible."

The theme

  • The herb counters: The Pharisees tithed even their spices. Jesus doesn't mock the care; he mourns what's missing.
  • Justice and love: "These you should have done, without overlooking the others." The big things come first.
  • One finger: The scholars of the law laid burdens on others and wouldn't lift a finger. Faith that doesn't help isn't faith.
  • The fruit of the Spirit: Paul's nine-part list is a portrait of a life where God is at work: not rules kept, but fruit grown.

Yesterday it was the inside of the cup. Today Jesus names what the inside should produce: justice, love, and help for people carrying heavy loads.

1. Mint, Rue and Every Garden Herb

"Woe to you Pharisees! You pay tithes of mint and of rue and of every garden herb, but you pay no attention to judgment and to love for God."

  • Careful Giving: The Law asked for a tenth of the harvest. Some Pharisees extended it to the smallest herbs, a sign of real devotion.
  • The Missing Center: The problem isn't the tithe. It's paying attention to the herbs and none to justice and the love of God.

2. The Best Seats

"Woe to you Pharisees! You love the seat of honor in synagogues and greetings in marketplaces."

  • Seen by Others: The second woe is about image: honor, titles, recognition. It's yesterday's clean outside of the cup.
  • Unmarked Graves: Jesus adds that they are "like unseen graves" that people walk over without knowing (11:44). Touching a grave made a Jew ritually impure. The ones who seem cleanest, Jesus says, are quietly making others unclean.

3. Burdens Hard to Carry

"Woe also to you scholars of the law! You impose on people burdens hard to carry, but you yourselves do not lift one finger to touch them."

  • Rules for Others: The experts made religion heavier for ordinary people and offered no help with the weight.
  • Jesus's Opposite: Elsewhere Jesus says, "Come to me, all you who labor and are burdened... my yoke is easy, and my burden light" (Matthew 11:28–30).

4. Works of the Flesh

Paul lists what grows from a life ruled by selfishness: "immorality, impurity, licentiousness, idolatry, sorcery, hatreds, rivalry, jealousy, outbursts of fury, acts of selfishness, dissensions, factions, occasions of envy, drinking bouts, orgies, and the like."

  • Mostly Relational: Most of the list isn't about the body at all. It's about what destroys community: rivalry, jealousy, factions.
  • Works, Plural: Paul calls them "works," things we produce. They multiply and scatter.

5. The Fruit of the Spirit

"In contrast, the fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, generosity, faithfulness, gentleness, self-control. Against such there is no law." Fruit isn't manufactured; it grows. A tree doesn't strain to produce apples; it stays rooted and the apples come, like the tree planted by streams of water in Psalm 1. "If we live in the Spirit, let us also follow the Spirit." Jesus's woes and Paul's fruit are two sides of one teaching: God isn't asking for more careful rule-keeping, but for a life rooted in him that grows love and lifts other people's burdens.

For Christians today

The fruit of the Spirit is one of the most loved passages in the New Testament, memorized by children and used as a checklist for the spiritual life.

  • The oldest readings: Augustine pointed out that love comes first in Paul's list, and that the other eight are love in different forms: joy is love rejoicing, peace is love at rest, patience is love enduring. Early Christians also noted that Paul wrote "fruit," not "fruits."
  • Catholic: Catholic tradition counts twelve fruits of the Holy Spirit, following the Latin Vulgate, which adds goodness, modesty and chastity (Catechism §1832). They're distinct from the seven gifts of the Spirit from Isaiah 11, which Catholics receive at confirmation.
  • Protestant and evangelical: Many Protestant traditions use the fruit of the Spirit as the main measure of spiritual growth. Pentecostals pair it with the gifts of the Spirit (1 Corinthians 12), teaching that gifts without fruit are hollow. Luther taught that good works flow from faith like fruit from a good tree.
  • Orthodox: St. Seraphim of Sarov (1759–1833) famously taught that the true aim of the Christian life is "the acquisition of the Holy Spirit," and that prayer, fasting and good works are means to that end, not the end itself.
  • Live debates today: Jesus's woes have often been used to caricature Judaism as legalistic. Many churches now teach these passages as warnings to Christian leaders: clergy who load burdens on others, religious communities more focused on rules than on justice. The phrase "lift a finger" has also shaped Christian debates on how churches respond to poverty and need.

Carry it today: Pick one fruit from Paul's list that's weakest in you right now, and practice it once on purpose today.

StoicismWiki ↗

What is Stoicism? A philosophy founded by Zeno of Citium around 300 BC in Athens, named for the painted porch (stoa) where he taught. Its most famous teachers are Epictetus, born into slavery; Seneca, a Roman statesman; and the emperor Marcus Aurelius. The Stoics taught that we can't control events, only our judgments and choices. Their four virtues are wisdom, courage, justice and self-control.

Marcus Aurelius praises the person who does good without fuss, like "a vine which has produced grapes, and seeks for nothing more after it has once produced its proper fruit." (Meditations 5.6, Long translation.) Marcus says some people do a kindness and then keep score; others at least remember it as a debt. But the best do good the way a vine bears grapes, then move on to the next season. That's Paul's fruit of the Spirit in Stoic dress: good that grows naturally, without seeking credit or the best seat.

Today's readings through Hindu and Jain eyes: Kushmanda and selfless action.

  • The Hindu lens: Today is the fourth day of Sharad Navaratri, which honors Kushmanda, the Goddess who, tradition says, created the universe with her smile and lights it from within. Wednesday and Chaturthi (the fourth lunar day) both belong to Ganesha, remover of obstacles, the god who helps carry life's burdens. The Gita's ideal of karma yoga, selfless action for others' good, is the Hindu answer to "lift a finger."
  • The Jain lens: Jains teach that a person is known by conduct, not position or ritual. Right conduct (samyak charitra) is one of the "three jewels," with right faith and right knowledge. All three together lead to freedom; any one alone is incomplete.

Today is Shukla Chaturthi (Choth), the fourth day of the bright half, with the moon in Vishakha, moving into Anuradha (per Indian calendars; exact Chicago times couldn't be confirmed).

What the words mean (all Sanskrit):

  • Kushmanda: often read as ku, "little," ushma, "warmth," and anda, "egg": "she who made the cosmic egg with a little warmth" (one traditional reading among several).
  • Karma yoga: karma, "action," plus yoga, "union, discipline": the path of selfless action.
  • Charitra: "conduct, behavior," from car, "to move, to walk."
  • Chaturthi: "the fourth" (from catur, "four," compare Latin quattuor).
  • Anuradha: "following Radha," or "the one after Vishakha," the nakshatra of friendship and devotion.

Today's readings through a Jewish lens: the weightier matters.

  • What God requires: The prophet Micah sums up the Law: "Only to do justice and to love goodness, and to walk humbly with your God" (Micah 6:8). The Talmud (Makkot 24a) describes how the 613 commandments were boiled down by later prophets, Micah to three, and finally Habakkuk to one: the righteous live by their faith (Habakkuk 2:4).

The time. Jesus's ministry was around AD 28–30; Luke wrote around AD 80–90 and Paul wrote Galatians around AD 50–55. Tithing went back to the Law of Moses (Leviticus 27:30; Deuteronomy 14:22), which required a tenth of grain, wine and oil.

What scholars broadly agree on. The Mishnah, a Jewish law code compiled around AD 200, shows rabbis debating which plants needed tithing, and it lists rue among those exempt from tithes (Shevi'it 9:1). That makes Jesus's mention of rue striking: Luke may be showing Pharisees going beyond even what their own tradition required. Matthew's version (23:23) lists "mint and dill and cummin" and names "judgment and mercy and fidelity" as "the weightier things of the law."

What it meant then. Jesus's call to justice and the love of God echoes the prophets: Micah's "Only to do justice and to love goodness, and to walk humbly with your God" (Micah 6:8). The scholars of the law were experts who interpreted the Law for ordinary people. Many of their rulings were meant to protect the commandments, but they could become a heavy load for the poor, who couldn't afford to keep every detail.

The allegorical readings. Medieval writers read the herbs as small virtues and the "weightier matters" as the great ones, and read the unmarked graves as hypocrisy hidden under respectability. Augustine and others read the nine fruits as a ladder, beginning with love and ending with self-control.

What's debated or unknown. Scholars debate how much Luke's portrait reflects actual Pharisees of Jesus's day, and how much it reflects later conflict between early Christians and the synagogue. The order and number of the fruits differ in some ancient manuscripts, which is why some traditions count nine and others twelve.
