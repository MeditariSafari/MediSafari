October 13th, 2026The five-minute version

The readings: Jesus is invited to dinner at a Pharisee's house. He sits down to eat without doing the ritual handwashing, and his host is shocked. Jesus says that they carefully clean the outside of the cup and the dish, but inside they are full of greed and wickedness. "Did not the maker of the outside also make the inside?" The way to be clean, he says, is to give from the inside: generosity to the poor. In Galatians, Paul tells a church that's tempted to add rules to their faith that the rules don't count. What counts is "faith working through love."

What it means: It's easy to get the outside right: the right habits, the right image, the right words. Jesus cares about the inside. God made both, and he wants both clean. Paul says the same thing from another angle: faith isn't a set of boxes to check, it's trust in God that shows up as love. Yesterday's readings asked us to stop waiting for a sign. Today's ask us to stop polishing the outside of the cup.

Across the traditions: the same truth, in other words.

  • Buddha: "Within thee there is ravening, but the outside thou makest clean."
  • Tao Te Ching: the great man "dwells with the fruit and not with the flower."
  • Confucius: "Fine words and an insinuating appearance are seldom associated with true virtue."
  • Bahá'í: "Possess a pure, kindly and radiant heart."
  • Guru Nanak: water washes the body; only the Name washes the mind.

Carry it today: Where are you cleaning the outside of the cup while leaving the inside untouched?

The theme

  • The outside of the cup: Jesus isn't against washing; he's against a religion that stops at the surface.
  • The maker of both: "Did not the maker of the outside also make the inside?" God wants the whole of us, not just the part people see.
  • Give from within: Jesus's cure for a dirty heart is generosity. Giving cleans the giver.
  • Faith working through love: Paul's test for real faith is simple: is it making you more loving?

Yesterday Jesus refused to give the crowd a sign. Today, at a dinner table, he shows what he's looking for instead: a heart that's clean on the inside.

1. A Dinner Invitation

Luke says a Pharisee "invited him to dine at his home." Jesus eats with everyone in Luke's Gospel: tax collectors, sinners, and Pharisees too.

  • Jesus Accepts: He doesn't avoid the people who disagree with him. He sits at their table.
  • The Shock: The host "was amazed to see that he did not observe the prescribed washing before the meal." Jesus seems to skip it on purpose, to start a conversation.

2. The Outside of the Cup

"Oh you Pharisees! Although you cleanse the outside of the cup and the dish, inside you are filled with plunder and evil."

  • Ritual, Not Hygiene: The washing was a religious practice, a way of setting the meal apart as holy. It was a good practice that could become a performance.
  • Plunder and Evil: The Greek word for "plunder" means greed or robbery. Jesus is pointing at what fills the cup: what we've grabbed, not what we've given.

3. The Maker of Both

"You fools! Did not the maker of the outside also make the inside?"

  • One Creator: The God who cares about the body also cares about the heart. Religion that tends only one of them is half a religion.
  • No Hiding Place: We can manage what others see. We can't manage what God sees.

4. Give Alms from Within

"But as to what is within, give alms, and behold, everything will be clean for you."

  • Generosity Cleans: Jesus offers a surprising cure: not more scrubbing, but giving. Generosity empties a heart full of grabbing.
  • What Is Within: The phrase is hard to translate. It can mean "give what's inside the cup to the poor," or "give from the heart." Both point the same way.

5. Faith Working Through Love

Paul writes to churches being told that real Christians must also keep the rules of the Law, starting with circumcision. He says no: "For in Christ Jesus, neither circumcision nor uncircumcision counts for anything, but only faith working through love." Jesus and Paul are saying the same thing in different settings. Outward marks, whether washing or circumcision or anything we do to look religious, can't make us clean. Faith that trusts God and shows itself in love can. The question for today isn't how clean we look, but what our faith is doing.

For Christians today

"Faith working through love" (Galatians 5:6) became a meeting point in one of the oldest arguments in Christianity: faith versus works.

  • The oldest readings: Augustine loved Galatians 5:6, reading it as proof that saving faith is never alone; it always loves. John Chrysostom, preaching on Luke's dinner scene, said God asks for clean souls more than clean hands.
  • Catholic: The Council of Trent cited Galatians 5:6 to teach that faith must be "formed by love" to justify. The Catechism teaches that the moral life begins in the heart, where our choices are born (§1968, §2517).
  • Protestant and evangelical: Luther read Galatians 5:6 as describing the fruit of faith, not its cause: we're saved by faith alone, but saving faith is never alone; it always shows itself in love. Wesley built his teaching on holiness of heart on the same idea.
  • Orthodox: The Orthodox Church speaks of purifying the heart (katharsis) as the first stage of the spiritual life, before illumination and union with God. "Blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God" (Matthew 5:8) is central.
  • Live debates today: In 1999, the Catholic Church and the Lutheran World Federation signed the Joint Declaration on the Doctrine of Justification, agreeing that we're saved by grace through faith and that good works follow from it, easing a dispute that began in 1517. Methodists, Anglicans and Reformed churches have since joined it. Scholars also ask whether Jesus's criticism of the Pharisees has been used unfairly to paint all Judaism as empty ritual (see Historical context).

Carry it today: Do one generous thing today that no one will see.

StoicismWiki ↗

What is Stoicism? A philosophy founded by Zeno of Citium around 300 BC in Athens, named for the painted porch (stoa) where he taught. Its most famous teachers are Epictetus, born into slavery; Seneca, a Roman statesman; and the emperor Marcus Aurelius. The Stoics taught that we can't control events, only our judgments and choices. Their four virtues are wisdom, courage, justice and self-control.

"Men are disturbed, not by things, but by the principles and notions which they form concerning things." (Epictetus, Enchiridion 5, Carter translation.) Epictetus taught that the source of our trouble is inside, in our judgments, not outside in events. When we're upset, he said, we should look to our own opinions instead of blaming others. That's the Stoic version of today's Gospel: the outside of the cup isn't the problem; the inside is.

Today's readings through Hindu and Jain eyes: Chandraghanta and inner purity.

  • The Hindu lens: Today is the third day of Sharad Navaratri, which honors Chandraghanta, the Goddess with a half-moon shaped like a bell on her forehead. She stands for courage and peace together, and her bell is said to drive away evil. The Hindu tradition prizes inner purity (shaucha) as one of the basic disciplines of yoga, the purity of mind as well as body.
  • The Jain lens: Jains teach that true purity is freedom from the passions they call kashayas: anger, pride, deceit and greed. Ritual without inner change, they say, doesn't free the soul. Greed, the "plunder" Jesus names today, is the last and hardest to give up.

Today is Shukla Tritiya (Trij), the third day of the bright half, with the moon in Vishakha (per Indian calendars; exact Chicago times couldn't be confirmed). Tuesday belongs to Hanuman and the planet Mars (Mangala).

What the words mean (all Sanskrit):

  • Chandraghanta: chandra, "moon," plus ghaṇṭā, "bell": "she who has a moon-bell."
  • Shaucha: "purity, cleanness," from śuci, "bright, clean."
  • Kashaya: literally "stain, dye": the passions that color the soul.
  • Tritiya: "the third" (from tri, "three," compare Latin tres).
  • Vishakha: "forked, branched," a nakshatra in the constellation Libra.

What scholars broadly agree on.

The time. Jesus's ministry was around AD 28–30; Luke wrote around AD 80–90 and Paul wrote Galatians around AD 50–55. The Pharisees were a Jewish renewal movement that began in the 2nd century BC. After the destruction of the Temple in AD 70, their tradition helped shape rabbinic Judaism.

What scholars broadly agree on. The Pharisees wanted ordinary Jews to live with the holiness of priests, and extending priestly handwashing to everyday meals was part of that. Washing before meals was becoming customary in Jesus's day, though scholars debate how widely it was practiced. Luke's version of this dispute shares material with Matthew 23, so it likely comes from a common source of Jesus's sayings.

What it meant then. Arguments about purity were normal among Jews in this period, and Jesus was arguing as a Jew inside his own tradition. The prophets had already said that God desires a clean heart more than sacrifice (Psalm 51; Hosea 6:6). For Paul's Gentile converts in Galatia, the argument was about whether non-Jews had to become Jews to follow Jesus. Paul's answer, no, shaped the whole future of the Church.

The allegorical readings. Early writers read the cup as the human person: the outside the body, the inside the soul. Some medieval preachers read the alms of verse 41 as mercy of every kind, not just money: forgiveness, patience and kindness count too.

What's debated or unknown. Verse 41 is one of the hardest lines in Luke to translate. "What is within" may mean "the contents of the cup" or "your inner life," and some scholars think an Aramaic word for "cleanse" was misread as "give alms." Scholars today also stress that the Gospels show the Pharisees through the lens of later conflict; Jewish sources show many Pharisees teaching the same inner piety Jesus calls for.
