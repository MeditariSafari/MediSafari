“The land is good and God is with us, but fear makes giants of our problems and grasshoppers of ourselves.”
October 21st, 2026The five-minute version

The readings: The Israelites are finally at the border of the promised land. Moses sends twelve scouts. They come back carrying a cluster of grapes so big it takes two men and a pole. "It flows with milk and honey," they say, "yet the people who live in the land are strong." Caleb says, "Let's go. We can do this." But ten of the scouts say no: the people are giants, "and to ourselves we seemed like grasshoppers." The whole camp panics and wants to elect a new leader and go back to slavery in Egypt. Joshua and Caleb beg them: the land is good, God is with us, don't be afraid. In Matthew, Jesus tells his friends he'll be killed and raised, and they're crushed. Then the Temple tax collectors come, and Jesus sends Peter fishing: the first fish will have a coin in its mouth, enough to pay for both of them.

What it means: Ten scouts and Caleb saw the same land, the same grapes and the same giants. The difference was what they believed about God, and about themselves. Fear shrank them into grasshoppers before anyone else did. Going back to Egypt looked safer than trusting God in the unknown. Jesus' disciples were afraid too, but Jesus shows that God provides, even a coin in a fish. The question for us: what giant are we facing, and are we measuring it against ourselves or against God?

Across the traditions: the same truth, in other words.

  • Seneca: "There are more things, Lucilius, likely to frighten us than there are to crush us; we suffer more often in imagination than in reality."
  • Buddha: Taught that one who is awake and free from craving has nothing to fear (Dhammapada, paraphrased).
  • Tao Te Ching: Taught that the journey of a thousand miles begins with a single step (Ch. 64, paraphrased).
  • Bhagavad Gita: Krishna tells Arjuna, frozen with fear on the battlefield, to stand up and not yield to this unmanliness (Ch. 2, paraphrased).
  • Islam: the Qur'an says, "Do not lose heart and do not grieve, for you will have the upper hand if you are believers" (3:139, paraphrased).

Carry it today: What giant has made you feel like a grasshopper, and what would Caleb say about it?

The theme

  • Same land, two reports: All twelve scouts saw the milk, the honey and the strong cities. Ten saw only giants.
  • Grasshoppers: "To ourselves we seemed like grasshoppers, and so we seemed to them." How we see ourselves shapes how others see us.
  • Back to Egypt: Fear makes slavery look safe. The people want a new leader to take them back.
  • Do not fear: Joshua and Caleb: "If the Lord is pleased with us, he will bring us into this land."

This week the RCL has followed Israel from the finished tabernacle to God's glory filling it, to jealousy in Moses' family, and now to the border of the promised land. Fear, not giants, keeps them out for forty years.

1. Milk and Honey, and Giants

"We came to the land to which you sent us; it flows with milk and honey, and this is its fruit. Yet the people who live in the land are strong."

  • A True Report: The scouts don't lie about the fruit. The land is everything God promised.
  • The Word "Yet": One small word turns the report from gift to threat. Fear often starts with "yet."

2. We Are Well Able

Caleb: "Let us go up at once and occupy it, for we are well able to overcome it." The others: "We are not able to go up against this people, for they are stronger than we."

  • Same Facts, Different Faith: Caleb didn't deny the giants. He measured them against God.
  • A Different Spirit: God later says Caleb "has a different spirit and has followed me wholeheartedly" (14:24).

3. Like Grasshoppers

"And to ourselves we seemed like grasshoppers, and so we seemed to them."

  • Self-Image: They decided they were small before the giants ever looked at them. The rabbis noticed: how could they know how they seemed to the giants? They projected their own fear.
  • Contagious: Ten frightened voices outweighed two faithful ones, and the whole camp wept all night.

4. Back to Egypt

"Would that we had died in the land of Egypt! Or would that we had died in this wilderness!" "Let us choose a captain and go back to Egypt." Joshua and Caleb answer: "The land that we went through as spies is an exceedingly good land. If the Lord is pleased with us, he will bring us into this land and give it to us, a land that flows with milk and honey. Only, do not rebel against the Lord; and do not fear the people of the land."

  • The Pull of the Familiar: Slavery was terrible, but it was known. Freedom is uncertain.
  • Bread for Us: Joshua and Caleb say the giants are "no more than bread for us," a bold image of trust.

5. A Coin in a Fish

"What do you think, Simon? From whom do kings of the earth take toll or tribute? From their children or from others?" "Then the children are free. However, so that we do not give offense to them, go to the sea and cast a hook; take the first fish that comes up, and when you open its mouth you will find a coin; take that and give it to them for you and me." The disciples had just heard Jesus speak of his death and were "greatly distressed," another camp afraid of what lay ahead. Jesus answers their fear not with a speech but with a strange, almost playful provision: a coin in a fish's mouth, enough for Jesus and Peter both. The children of God are free, and God provides. Israel at the border couldn't believe God would provide; Caleb could. The scouts' fear cost a generation forty years; Caleb and Joshua, the only two who trusted, are the only two of that generation who enter the land.

For Christians today

The spies have been preached on as a lesson in faith over fear for centuries, and the coin in the fish has puzzled readers for just as long.

  • The oldest readings: The fathers read Joshua (whose name in Greek is Iēsous, Jesus) as a figure of Christ who leads God's people into the promised land. Hebrews 3–4 uses the wilderness generation as a warning: "they were unable to enter because of unbelief" (3:19, paraphrased).
  • Catholic: Catholic tradition calls fortitude, courage in the face of difficulty, one of the cardinal virtues and a gift of the Holy Spirit (Catechism §1808, §1831). Today's Gospel, "to whom much has been given, much will be required," fits Caleb's responsibility.
  • Protestant and evangelical: Evangelical preaching often uses "grasshopper mentality" to describe low self-worth and fear. Calvin read the spies as a lesson that unbelief, not the enemy, is the true obstacle.
  • Orthodox: Orthodox tradition honors Joshua and Caleb as righteous men, and reads the promised land as the kingdom of heaven reached through faith and perseverance.
  • Live debates today: The conquest of Canaan raises hard questions about violence in Scripture, which many Christians wrestle with. And the temple tax story is debated: does it teach paying taxes and keeping peace with institutions, or the freedom of God's children from them?

Carry it today: Name one fear honestly, then name one way God has provided before.

StoicismWiki ↗

What is Stoicism? A philosophy founded by Zeno of Citium around 300 BC in Athens, named for the painted porch (stoa) where he taught. Its most famous teachers are Epictetus, born into slavery; Seneca, a Roman statesman; and the emperor Marcus Aurelius. The Stoics taught that we can't control events, only our judgments and choices. Their four virtues are wisdom, courage, justice and self-control.

"There are more things, Lucilius, likely to frighten us than there are to crush us; we suffer more often in imagination than in reality." (Seneca, Moral Letters to Lucilius 13.) Seneca's line could be the motto of the ten scouts. The giants were real, but the grasshopper fear was imagined, and it cost them more than the giants ever could. Seneca's advice: separate what you know from what you fear, and face only what's actually in front of you today.

The time. Numbers is set around the 13th century BC; the spy story combines older and later sources, shaped into its final form around the Persian period. The temple tax story is set around AD 29–30 in Capernaum.

What scholars broadly agree on. The temple tax was a half-shekel paid yearly by Jewish men for the upkeep of the Jerusalem Temple (Exodus 30:13). After the Temple was destroyed in AD 70, Rome redirected the tax to the temple of Jupiter in Rome, which made Matthew's story pointed for his readers. Kadesh, where the scouts returned, is usually identified with Ein el-Qudeirat in the northeastern Sinai. Hebron, which the scouts visited, was an ancient Canaanite city in the hills of Judah.

What it meant then. For exiles hoping to return to the land after Babylon, the spy story asked whether they would trust God or fear the obstacles. For Matthew's church, paying the tax "so that we do not give offense" meant keeping peace with fellow Jews while knowing they were free children of God.

The allegorical readings. The fathers saw the great cluster of grapes carried on a pole by two men as a figure of Christ on the cross. The coin in the fish was read as Christ providing for his church, and the fish as a symbol of Christ himself (the Greek ichthys).

What's debated or unknown. Scholars debate whether the "giants" (the Anakim) reflect memories of an actual people, or legendary tall ancestors. They also debate whether the coin-in-the-fish was meant as a miracle, a figure of speech ("go fish and sell the catch"), or a playful saying. Matthew never says Peter caught the fish.
