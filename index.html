October 26th, 2026The five-minute version

The readings: The RCL takes a short, quiet step back today. Numbers records a single moment: in the fortieth year, Aaron, Moses' older brother and Israel's first high priest, climbs Mount Hor and dies there, at 123. The two brothers who led Israel out of Egypt both die within sight of the end. Psalm 119 sings that God's law brings freedom: "I shall walk at liberty, for I have sought your precepts." And James, writing to a church that seats the rich in the best places and the poor on the floor, says the "royal law" is "love your neighbor as yourself." Playing favorites breaks it. He ends: "Mercy triumphs over judgment."

What it means: Aaron was the priest: the one who offered sacrifices for the people's sins and blessed them in God's name. James says the true law is a law of liberty, and its heart is mercy. The law isn't a weapon for judging others; it's the way of love that sets us free. Whoever shows mercy will receive it.

Across the traditions: the same truth, in other words.

  • Marcus Aurelius: of the difficult people he'd meet each day, "nor can I be angry with my kinsman, nor hate him, For we are made for co-operation." (Meditations 2.1, Long.)
  • Pirkei Avot: Hillel said to be disciples of Aaron, loving peace and pursuing peace, loving people and drawing them near to the Torah (Avot 1:12, paraphrased).
  • Islam: Every chapter of the Qur'an but one begins "In the name of God, the Most Merciful, the Most Compassionate," and a hadith says God's mercy prevails over His wrath (Bukhari, paraphrased).
  • Buddha: Today is Pavāraṇā, when monks invite one another to point out their faults, received with gratitude, not judgment.
  • Tao Te Ching: The sage is good to the good, and good to those who aren't good too, and so goodness is gained (Ch. 49, paraphrased).

Carry it today: Where are you quick to judge, and what would mercy look like there?

The theme

  • Aaron's death: Israel's first priest, the one who blessed the people, dies on the mountain.
  • Liberty: Psalm 119 sees God's law not as a cage but as freedom: "I shall walk at liberty."
  • The royal law: James names love of neighbor the law of the King.
  • Mercy wins: "Mercy triumphs over judgment." The last word belongs to mercy.

All week the RCL followed Moses to his death on Nebo. Today it remembers his brother Aaron, who died first, on Mount Hor. Tomorrow the readings move on.

1. Aaron on Mount Hor

"Aaron the priest went up Mount Hor at the command of the Lord and died there, in the fortieth year after the Israelites had come out of the land of Egypt."

  • The Brother: Aaron spoke for Moses before Pharaoh, held up his arms in battle, and served as the first high priest.
  • Not Perfect: Aaron also made the golden calf. God still made him a priest. Mercy, not perfection, qualified him.

2. Walking at Liberty

"I shall walk at liberty, for I have sought your precepts." "I find my delight in your commandments because I love them."

  • Freedom Through Law: For the psalmist, God's commands don't confine; they open a wide road.
  • Delight: The psalm loves the law the way you love a person. Obedience flows from love.

3. The Royal Law

"If you really fulfill the royal law according to the scripture, 'You shall love your neighbor as yourself.'"

  • Royal: It's the King's law, and the law that rules all others. Jesus called it second only to loving God.
  • No Favorites: James wrote against seating the rich in honor and the poor on the floor. Favoritism breaks the royal law.

4. The Whole Law

"For whoever keeps the whole law but fails in one point has become accountable for all of it."

  • One Fabric: The law isn't a list to score points on; it's one piece. Tear it anywhere and it's torn.
  • Humility: No one keeps it perfectly, so no one can look down on anyone else.

5. Mercy Triumphs Over Judgment

"So speak and so act as those who are to be judged by the law of liberty. For judgment will be without mercy to anyone who has shown no mercy; mercy triumphs over judgment." James' final line could stand over Aaron's whole life. The man who built the golden calf became the priest who carried the names of Israel's tribes on his heart into God's presence. In the Catholic Gospel today, Jesus heals a woman on the Sabbath, choosing a suffering person over a rule; the synagogue leader chooses the rule. James says the same choice faces us every day, in how we speak and act. The law of liberty isn't looser than the old rules; it's deeper. It asks us to treat others as God has treated us.

For Christians today

"Mercy triumphs over judgment" is one of James' most quoted lines, and James himself has long been debated.

  • The oldest readings: The fathers saw Aaron as a type of Christ the high priest (Hebrews 5:4–5), and Aaron's death before entering the land as a sign that the old priesthood would give way to Christ's.
  • Catholic: Pope Francis made mercy a central theme of his papacy, declaring a Jubilee Year of Mercy (2015–2016) and calling mercy "the very foundation of the Church's life." The Catechism's corporal and spiritual works of mercy put James into practice.
  • Protestant and evangelical: Luther famously called James "an epistle of straw" because it seemed to stress works over faith, though he still kept it in his Bible. Many Protestants today read James and Paul together: true faith shows itself in love.
  • Orthodox: The Orthodox stress God's philanthropia, love for humankind, and pray "Lord, have mercy" constantly in the liturgy. St. Demetrios, honored today, is remembered as a protector of his city.
  • Live debates today: How churches balance mercy and moral standards, on everything from divorce to immigration, is argued across traditions.

Carry it today: Speak to someone you've judged as if you were the one being judged.

StoicismWiki ↗

What is Stoicism? A philosophy founded by Zeno of Citium around 300 BC in Athens, named for the painted porch (stoa) where he taught. Its most famous teachers are Epictetus, born into slavery; Seneca, a Roman statesman; and the emperor Marcus Aurelius. The Stoics taught that we can't control events, only our judgments and choices. Their four virtues are wisdom, courage, justice and self-control.

"Begin the morning by saying to thyself, I shall meet with the busy-body, the ungrateful, arrogant, deceitful, envious, unsocial. All these things happen to them by reason of their ignorance of what is good and evil... I can neither be injured by any of them... nor can I be angry with my kinsman, nor hate him, For we are made for co-operation, like feet, like hands, like eyelids." (Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 2.1, Long translation.) The most powerful man in the world prepared each morning to meet difficult people, not with judgment but with understanding: they act from ignorance, and they are still kin. It's a Stoic version of James' royal law: before you judge, remember you belong to each other.

Today's readings through Hindu and Jain eyes: Valmiki and mercy.

  • The Hindu lens: Today is Valmiki Jayanti, honoring the sage who wrote the Ramayana. Tradition says Valmiki was once a highway robber who was transformed by meeting holy sages and repeating Rama's name. A sinner became the first poet: mercy over judgment.
  • The Jain lens: Jains practice kshama, forgiveness, as the first of the ten virtues; the annual festival of Paryushana ends with asking forgiveness of all beings: Micchami Dukkadam, "may my faults be forgiven."

Today is Purnima, the full moon (ending at 9:41 AM India time), then Krishna Pratipada, the first day of the waning moon, with the moon moving from Ashvini into Bharani (per Indian calendars; exact Chicago times couldn't be confirmed).

What the words mean (all Sanskrit or Prakrit):

  • Valmiki: "born of an anthill," because legend says he meditated so long an anthill grew over him.
  • Kshama: "forgiveness, patience."
  • Micchami Dukkadam: Prakrit, "may my wrongdoing be fruitless."
  • Bharani: "the bearer."

The time. Numbers sets Aaron's death in the fortieth year of the wilderness journey, traditionally the 13th century BC. The Letter of James is dated anywhere from the 40s to the 90s AD; many scholars place it late in the first century.

What scholars broadly agree on. Numbers 33 is an itinerary listing Israel's camps, likely compiled from older sources by priestly writers. Mount Hor's location is uncertain; tradition places it near Petra in Jordan, where a shrine to Aaron (Jebel Harun) still stands. James is written in good Greek and draws heavily on Jewish wisdom and on sayings of Jesus, especially the Sermon on the Mount.

What it meant then. For Israel, Aaron's death marked the end of the Exodus generation's leaders and the handing on of the priesthood to his son Eleazar. For James' readers, mostly poor Jewish Christians, the letter was a call to treat the poor with honor and to live their faith, not just profess it.

The allegorical readings. The fathers read Aaron's priestly garments, bearing the names of the tribes over his heart, as Christ carrying humanity before God.

What's debated or unknown. Who wrote James is debated: James the brother of Jesus, or a later writer in his name. How James' "faith without works is dead" fits with Paul's teaching on faith has been argued since the Reformation.
