October 20th, 2026The five-minute version

The readings: Moses' own brother and sister turn on him. It starts as a complaint about his wife, a woman from Cush (likely Ethiopia), but the real grievance comes out fast: "Has the Lord spoken only through Moses? Has he not spoken through us also?" The story pauses to tell us Moses was the humblest man on earth; he doesn't defend himself. God does. God calls the three of them out and says that with other prophets he speaks in dreams and visions, but with Moses "face to face." Miriam is struck with a skin disease, and Moses, the one they attacked, prays for her healing. In Revelation, an angel throws a boulder into the sea: that's how Babylon will be thrown down, and its music, craftsmen and wedding songs will be heard no more.

What it means: Jealousy is the fear that someone else's gift takes something from us. Miriam and Aaron were great leaders in their own right, but they couldn't stand that Moses had something they didn't. Moses doesn't fight back; he prays for the sister who wronged him. Babylon is jealousy and pride built into a city, always wanting more, always comparing, and it ends in silence. The question for us: whose gift or calling do we resent, and can we pray for them instead?

Across the traditions: the same truth, in other words.

  • Epictetus: "Is anyone preferred before you at an entertainment, or in a compliment, or in being admitted to a consultation? If these things are good, you ought to be glad that he has gotten them."
  • Buddha: Taught mudita, sympathetic joy, delight in the good fortune of others, as the cure for envy (paraphrased).
  • Tao Te Ching: Taught that the sage doesn't display himself, and therefore shines; doesn't compete, so no one can compete with him (Ch. 22, paraphrased).
  • Islam: a well-known hadith warns that envy consumes good deeds as fire consumes wood (Abu Dawud, paraphrased).
  • Gita: Krishna praises the devotee who is free of envy and kind to all beings (Ch. 12, paraphrased).

Carry it today: Whose gift or success are you tempted to resent, and can you pray for them instead?

The theme

  • The real complaint: It starts with Moses' wife and ends with "Has he not spoken through us also?" Jealousy often hides behind other grievances.
  • Very humble: Moses doesn't defend himself. The narrator says no one on earth was humbler.
  • Face to face: God defends Moses: "With him I speak face to face—clearly, not in riddles."
  • No more: Babylon, built on pride and rivalry, falls silent: no music, no craftsmen, no wedding songs.

Yesterday God's glory filled the tabernacle and Babylon's merchants mourned their fall. Today the trouble comes from inside the family, and Revelation finishes Babylon's funeral song. Pride divides siblings and topples empires alike.

1. Because of the Cushite Woman

"While they were at Hazeroth, Miriam and Aaron spoke against Moses because of the Cushite woman whom he had married."

  • A Foreign Wife: Cush usually means the land south of Egypt, modern Sudan and Ethiopia. Some think she was Zipporah, Moses' Midianite wife; others think she was a second wife.
  • Prejudice as Cover: Their first complaint targets an outsider. Scholars and preachers have long noticed that God's answer ignores the wife entirely and goes straight to the brothers' and sister's pride.

2. Has He Not Spoken Through Us Also?

"Has the Lord spoken only through Moses? Has he not spoken through us also?" And the Lord heard it.

  • Real Gifts: Miriam was a prophet (Exodus 15:20) and Aaron the high priest. Their gifts were real. Jealousy often grows in gifted people.
  • The Lord Heard It: They spoke against Moses, but God was listening. Gossip about others is never as private as we think.

3. Very Humble

"Now the man Moses was very humble, more so than anyone else on the face of the earth."

  • No Defense: Moses says nothing in his own defense. The humble don't need to win the argument.
  • Humble, Not Weak: This is the man who stood up to Pharaoh. Humility isn't timidity; it's not needing to be first.

4. Face to Face

"Not so with my servant Moses; he is faithful in all my house. With him I speak face to face—clearly, not in riddles; and he beholds the form of the Lord." "And the anger of the Lord was kindled against them, and he departed."

  • Faithful in All My House: God's highest praise for Moses isn't power or eloquence but faithfulness.
  • Echo: Hebrews 3:2–6 quotes this verse: Moses was faithful as a servant in God's house; Christ, as a son.

5. Babylon Will Be Thrown Down

"With such violence Babylon the great city will be thrown down," "and the sound of harpists and entertainers and of flutists and trumpeters will be heard in you no more," "for your merchants were the magnates of the earth, and all nations were deceived by your sorcery. And in you was found the blood of prophets and of saints." In the verses right after today's reading (Numbers 12:10–15), Miriam is struck with a skin disease, Aaron begs Moses for help, and Moses prays one of the shortest prayers in the Bible: "O God, please heal her." The people wait seven days for Miriam before moving on. A family torn by jealousy is healed by the prayer of the one they attacked. Babylon, a whole civilization built on rivalry and pride, has no such ending; its music stops forever. Jealousy can be healed when someone prays for the person who hurt them. Pride that never repents ends in silence.

For Christians today

Moses' humility has been held up as a model for leaders, and the Cushite wife has become an important text in conversations about race.

  • The oldest readings: The fathers read Moses' Cushite wife as a figure of the church gathered from the nations, a bride from outside Israel. They praised Moses' silence and his prayer for Miriam as the model of loving one's enemies.
  • Catholic: Catholic tradition calls envy one of the seven capital sins, sadness at another's good, and names its remedy as goodwill and humility (Catechism §2538–2540). Today's Catholic first reading, "he is our peace," speaks of walls broken down between peoples.
  • Protestant and evangelical: John Wesley and other Protestant preachers held up Moses as a model of meekness under criticism. Black preachers in the United States have long read the Cushite wife as an African woman in Moses' household, and God's defense of Moses as a rebuke to racial prejudice.
  • Orthodox: Orthodox tradition calls Moses "God-seer" and honors Miriam and Aaron as saints, despite this episode. Orthodox spirituality treats envy as one of the most dangerous passions, cured by humility and prayer for the one envied.
  • Live debates today: Scholars and churches debate how much the Cushite wife's ethnicity was the issue, and what the passage says about interracial marriage and prejudice. Another debate: how should churches handle rivalry among gifted leaders?

Carry it today: Pray by name for someone whose success you envy.

StoicismWiki ↗

What is Stoicism? A philosophy founded by Zeno of Citium around 300 BC in Athens, named for the painted porch (stoa) where he taught. Its most famous teachers are Epictetus, born into slavery; Seneca, a Roman statesman; and the emperor Marcus Aurelius. The Stoics taught that we can't control events, only our judgments and choices. Their four virtues are wisdom, courage, justice and self-control.

"Is anyone preferred before you at an entertainment, or in a compliment, or in being admitted to a consultation? If these things are good, you ought to be glad that he has gotten them; and if they are evil, don't be grieved that you have not gotten them." (Epictetus, Enchiridion 25, Carter translation.) Epictetus' logic disarms envy: if another's honor is truly good, rejoice for them; if it isn't, why want it? Miriam and Aaron wanted Moses' place, but Moses' gift was faithfulness, and that was open to them too.

Today's readings through Hindu and Jain eyes: Vijayadashami and the fall of Lanka.

  • The Hindu lens: Today is Vijayadashami (Dussehra), the victory of good over evil: Rama's defeat of Ravana and Durga's victory over the buffalo demon Mahishasura. Across North India, giant effigies of Ravana are burned. Ravana was learned and powerful, but his pride and desire for what wasn't his destroyed his golden city of Lanka, Babylon's Hindu twin. In Bengal, Durga's images are immersed in rivers as she returns home.
  • The Jain lens: Jains teach that pride (mana) is one of the four passions (with anger, deceit and greed) that bind the soul. Its cure is mardava, humility, "softness."

Today is Shukla Dashami, the tenth day of the bright half (Navami ends at 12:50 PM India time), with the moon moving from Shravana into Dhanishta (per Indian calendars; exact Chicago times couldn't be confirmed).

What the words mean (all Sanskrit):

  • Vijayadashami: vijaya, "victory," plus daśamī, "the tenth."
  • Dussehra: from daśa, "ten," plus hara, "taking away": the defeat of ten-headed Ravana.
  • Mardava: "softness, gentleness."
  • Mana: "pride, self-regard."
  • Dhanishta: "the wealthiest."

The time. Numbers is set in the wilderness after Sinai, traditionally around the 13th century BC; the book was shaped over centuries and completed around the time of the Persian period. Revelation was written around AD 95.

What scholars broadly agree on. "Cush" in the Bible usually means the kingdom south of Egypt, in modern Sudan, famed for its powerful Black rulers; Cushite pharaohs ruled Egypt in the 8th–7th centuries BC. Hazeroth was a stopping place in the Sinai wilderness. The millstone image in Revelation borrows from Jeremiah 51:63–64, where a scroll tied to a stone is thrown into the Euphrates: "Thus shall Babylon sink."

What it meant then. In the ancient world, prophets and priests competed for authority. Numbers 12 settles who speaks most fully for God. For Revelation's readers, Rome's silence meant an end to the empire's luxuries, and to its persecution.

The allegorical readings. Origen and others read the Cushite wife as the church of the Gentiles, "black and beautiful" like the bride of the Song of Songs (1:5). The millstone was read as the weight of sin, sinking pride.

What's debated or unknown. Scholars debate whether the Cushite woman is Zipporah (who came from Midian, but some link Midian with a region called Cushan, Habakkuk 3:7) or a second wife. They also debate why only Miriam is punished and not Aaron; some think she led the complaint, others see a double standard.
