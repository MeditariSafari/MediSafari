October 25th, 2026The five-minute version

The readings: The Torah ends today. Moses, 120 years old, still clear-eyed and strong, climbs Mount Nebo. God shows him the whole land he has walked toward for forty years, from the hills of Gilead to the western sea, and says: "I have let you see it with your eyes, but you shall not cross over there." Moses dies there, and God buries him; no one knows where. Joshua, full of the spirit Moses laid on him, leads the people on. In Matthew, a lawyer tests Jesus: which commandment is greatest? Jesus answers with two from Moses: love God with all your heart, soul and mind, and love your neighbor as yourself. Everything else hangs on these. Paul remembers how he cared for the Thessalonians, gently, like a nurse with her children, giving them not just a message but his own self.

What it means: Moses' whole life comes down to two things: he knew God face to face, and he spent himself for a difficult people. That's Jesus' summary too: love God, love your neighbor. Moses didn't get to finish the journey, and he didn't need to. A life of love is complete even when the work is not. Someone else carries it on.

Across the traditions: the same truth, in other words.

  • Seneca: quoting Hecato, "I can show you a philtre, compounded without drugs, herbs, or any witch's incantation: 'If you would be loved, love.'" (Letter 9, Gummere.)
  • Rabbi Hillel: asked to teach the whole Torah standing on one foot, he said, what is hateful to you, don't do to your neighbor; the rest is commentary, go and learn (Talmud, Shabbat 31a, paraphrased).
  • Confucius: asked for one word to live by, he answered "reciprocity": don't do to others what you wouldn't want done to you (Analects 15.24, paraphrased).
  • Islam: the Prophet said none of you truly believes until he loves for his brother what he loves for himself (Forty Hadith of Nawawi 13, paraphrased).
  • Buddha: taught mettā, loving-kindness, like a mother protecting her only child (Karaṇīya Mettā Sutta, paraphrased). Paul's "nurse tenderly caring."

Carry it today: Who is your neighbor today, and what would loving them as yourself look like?

The theme

  • Nebo: Moses climbs one last mountain and sees the whole promise spread out before him.
  • Face to face: The Torah's last word on Moses isn't his laws or miracles but his friendship with God.
  • Two commandments: Jesus sums up all of Moses in two loves.
  • Our own selves: Paul gave the Thessalonians not just teaching but his life.

All week the RCL followed Moses through his last days. Today the story ends where it began, with a man who met God and gave himself for others. Next week the readings move on with Joshua into the land.

1. Mount Nebo

"Then Moses went up from the plains of Moab to Mount Nebo, to the top of Pisgah, which is opposite Jericho," and God showed him the whole land.

  • The View: From Nebo, in today's Jordan, you can still see the Jordan Valley, Jericho and, on clear days, Jerusalem.
  • Seeing, Not Entering: "I have let you see it with your eyes, but you shall not cross over there." The promise is real, even for one who won't live to enter it.

2. Face to Face

"Moses was one hundred twenty years old when he died; his sight was unimpaired, and his vigor had not abated." "Never since has there arisen a prophet in Israel like Moses, whom the Lord knew face to face."

  • Not Worn Out: Moses didn't fade away; he died at the Lord's command, still strong.
  • An Unknown Grave: "No one knows his burial place to this day." No shrine for Moses; the people must follow God, not a tomb.

3. Handing On

"Joshua son of Nun was full of the spirit of wisdom because Moses had laid his hands on him."

  • Laying on Hands: The church still ordains with this gesture: a calling passed from one person to the next.
  • Unfinished Work: Moses didn't finish; Joshua did. Every faithful life is part of a longer story.

4. The Greatest Commandment

"You shall love the Lord your God with all your heart and with all your soul and with all your mind." "And a second is like it: 'You shall love your neighbor as yourself.' On these two commandments hang all the Law and the Prophets."

  • Moses' Words: Jesus quotes Deuteronomy 6:5 (the Shema) and Leviticus 19:18. His answer is pure Moses.
  • Like It: The second is "like" the first. Loving God and loving people can't be pulled apart.

5. Our Own Selves

"So deeply do we care for you that we are determined to share with you not only the gospel of God but also our own selves, because you have become very dear to us." Paul's words to the Thessalonians could be Moses' epitaph. Moses carried the people like a nurse carrying a baby (Numbers 11:12, paraphrased), argued with God for them, and gave them his whole life, without entering the land himself. Jesus finishes his debates in Matthew with a question no one can answer, "Whose son is he?", and then goes to give his own self on the cross. The greatest commandment isn't a rule to master but a life to give. Psalm 90 asks God to "prosper for us the work of our hands," even the work we won't live to see finished.

For Christians today

Jesus' summary of the law is one of the most quoted passages in Christianity, and Moses' death is a favorite image for faithful endings.

  • The oldest readings: Augustine summed up Christian ethics as "love, and do what you will." The fathers saw Joshua, whose name is the Hebrew form of "Jesus," leading the people into the land where Moses could not, as a picture of grace completing the law.
  • Catholic: The Catechism organizes the Ten Commandments under the two great commandments (§2055). Today's Catholic first reading, from Exodus, makes love of neighbor concrete: the stranger, widow and orphan.
  • Protestant and evangelical: Many churches mark Reformation Sunday today. Martin Luther King Jr.'s last speech, the night before he died in 1968, borrowed this chapter: "I've been to the mountaintop... I've seen the Promised Land. I may not get there with you."
  • Orthodox: Today's Gospel of the rich man and Lazarus is a stark picture of failing to love the neighbor at your gate. Moses is honored as a saint, and the Orthodox see his face-to-face meetings with God as a model of theosis, union with God.
  • Live debates today: Who counts as a "neighbor" (immigrants, enemies, strangers) is argued in churches and politics alike.

Carry it today: Do one thing today whose results you won't see, and do it in love.

StoicismWiki ↗

What is Stoicism? A philosophy founded by Zeno of Citium around 300 BC in Athens, named for the painted porch (stoa) where he taught. Its most famous teachers are Epictetus, born into slavery; Seneca, a Roman statesman; and the emperor Marcus Aurelius. The Stoics taught that we can't control events, only our judgments and choices. Their four virtues are wisdom, courage, justice and self-control.

"Hecato, says: 'I can show you a philtre, compounded without drugs, herbs, or any witch's incantation: If you would be loved, love.'" (Seneca, Letter 9, Gummere translation.) Seneca is writing about friendship: the wise person doesn't need friends to be happy, but loves to have them, and the only way to gain a friend is to be one. The Stoics believed all people are citizens of one world, kin to each other through reason. Jesus says the same in a deeper key: love your neighbor as yourself.

Today's readings through Hindu and Jain eyes: Sharad Purnima.

  • The Hindu lens: Today is Sharad Purnima, the brightest full moon of the year, when Krishna is said to have danced the Rasa Lila with the gopis, a picture of the soul's love for God.   • The Jain lens: Jains teach ahimsa, non-harm to every living being, and maitri, friendship toward all: "I forgive all beings; may all beings forgive me; I have friendship toward all" (paraphrased from the Jain forgiveness prayer).

Today is Shukla Chaturdashi (ending at 11:56 AM India time), then Purnima, the full moon, with the moon moving from Revati into Ashvini (per Indian calendars; exact Chicago times couldn't be confirmed).

What the words mean (all Sanskrit):

  • Rasa Lila: "the dance of divine love."
  • Purnima: "full moon."
  • Maitri: "friendship, loving-kindness."
  • Ashvini: named for the Ashvins, the twin horse-headed healers, first of the 27 lunar mansions.

The time. Deuteronomy sets Moses' death on the plains of Moab, east of the Jordan, traditionally in the 13th century BC. Matthew wrote around AD 80–90; 1 Thessalonians dates to about AD 50, making it likely the oldest book of the New Testament.

What scholars broadly agree on. Deuteronomy 34 was written to close the Torah and to explain why Israel's greatest prophet never entered the land. Jesus' pairing of Deuteronomy 6:5 and Leviticus 19:18 echoes Jewish teachers of his time; Hillel and others also summarized the Torah in one principle. Mount Nebo is traditionally identified with a ridge in Jordan, where a Byzantine church and monastery were built.

What it meant then. For Israel in exile, outside the land, Moses dying in Moab was a comfort: even the greatest prophet lived and died outside the promise, still faithful. For Matthew's readers, Jesus' answer showed him as a true interpreter of Moses, not his opponent.

The allegorical readings. Gregory of Nyssa ended his Life of Moses by calling Moses the "friend of God," and said that the goal of the spiritual life is friendship with God. Origen read Joshua leading the people into the land as Jesus leading souls into the kingdom.

What's debated or unknown. Jewish tradition debated who wrote the last verses of the Torah, describing Moses' own death; the Talmud suggests Joshua did (Bava Batra 15a). Where Moses was buried, as the text says, no one knows.
