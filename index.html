“Moses' last song is a song of memory: God found us, carried us like an eagle, fed us honey from the rock. Don't forget the Rock that bore you.”
October 23rd, 2026The five-minute version

The readings: Moses' farewell song. He calls on heaven and earth to listen, and prays that his words will fall like gentle rain. Then he sings what God has done: God found Israel in a howling desert, protected them like the apple of his eye, and carried them like a mother eagle who stirs up the nest and catches her young on her wings when they fall. God fed them honey and oil out of solid rock. And then the sad turn: "you were unmindful of the Rock that bore you; you forgot the God who gave you birth." Psalm 90, the prayer of Moses, asks God to make our brief lives count. Titus says God's grace has appeared for everyone, training us to live well while we wait.

What it means: The song is Moses' last gift: a way to remember. God knew the people would forget, so Moses gave them something to sing when they did. Its tenderest images are motherly, an eagle hovering over her nest, a God "who gave you birth," and its strongest is the Rock, steady and faithful. The danger isn't that God will forget us; it's that we'll forget God, and forget who we are.

Across the traditions: the same truth, in other words.

  • Marcus Aurelius: began his Meditations by remembering what he owed others: "From my grandfather Verus I learned good morals and the government of my temper."
  • Psalm 90: "Lord, you have been our dwelling place in all generations."
  • Buddha: Taught gratitude (kataññutā), remembering kindness received, as a mark of a good person (paraphrased).
  • Islam: the Qur'an often calls itself dhikr, "the reminder," and urges, "remember Me; I will remember you" (2:152, paraphrased).
  • Confucius: Taught honoring parents and ancestors, carrying on their work (Analects 1.11, paraphrased).

Carry it today: What has God carried you through that you've forgotten to remember?

The theme

  • Like rain: Moses prays his words will fall "like gentle rain on grass," soaking in slowly.
  • The Rock: "The Rock, his work is perfect." God is steady when everything else changes.
  • The eagle: God stirs the nest and catches us on the way down. Care and challenge together.
  • Forgetting: "You forgot the God who gave you birth." The song exists so we can remember.

All week the RCL has followed Moses from the finished tabernacle to his last words. Today he sings. Sunday's readings will tell of his death on Mount Nebo, looking out at the land.

1. Give Ear, O Heavens

"Give ear, O heavens, and I will speak; let the earth hear the words of my mouth. May my teaching drop like the rain, my speech condense like the dew, like gentle rain on grass, like showers on new growth."

  • Cosmic Witnesses: Heaven and earth are called to listen, as witnesses to God's covenant.
  • Gentle Teaching: Moses wants his words to soak in like rain on grass, not crash like a storm.

2. The Rock

"The Rock, his work is perfect, and all his ways are just."

  • A Favorite Name: "Rock" is used for God six times in this song. It means strength, shelter, faithfulness.
  • Earlier This Week: Moses was hidden in the cleft of the rock; Paul said the rock that followed Israel was Christ.

3. Remember the Days of Old

"Remember the days of old." God "sustained him in a desert land, in a howling wilderness waste; he shielded him, cared for him, guarded him as the apple of his eye."

  • Memory as Faith: Israel's faith is built on remembering what God has done.
  • The Apple of His Eye: The pupil, the most protected part of the body. That's how God guarded them.

4. Like an Eagle

"As an eagle stirs up its nest and hovers over its young," God spread his wings and carried them; "he nursed him with honey from the crags."

  • Stirring the Nest: Mother eagles push their young out to learn to fly, then catch them. God's care includes challenge.
  • Honey from the Rock: Wild bees nest in rock crevices. God brings sweetness from the hardest places.

5. You Forgot the God Who Gave You Birth

"You were unmindful of the Rock that bore you; you forgot the God who gave you birth." The song's most striking image puts two pictures together: God as Rock, solid and unchanging, and God as a mother in labor, the one "who gave you birth." The tragedy is forgetting. Israel will grow comfortable in the land and forget who carried them there. That's why God gave them a song: songs live in memory long after sermons fade. Titus says the same grace "has appeared, bringing salvation to all," training us to live in the present while we wait in hope. Psalm 90 asks for one thing: that God would teach us to count our days and prosper the work of our hands. The way to not forget the Rock is to keep singing the song.

For Christians today

The Song of Moses has been sung by Jews and Christians for millennia, and its image of God as mother has drawn renewed attention.

  • The oldest readings: Revelation 15:3 pictures the saints singing "the song of Moses, the servant of God, and the song of the Lamb." The fathers read "the Rock" as Christ (1 Corinthians 10:4) and the eagle as God's providence in Christ.
  • Catholic: The Song of Moses is sung as a canticle in the Liturgy of the Hours (Saturday Morning Prayer, Week II). Catholic tradition has long noted maternal images of God; the Catechism says God's love is also expressed "through the image of motherhood" (§239).
  • Protestant and evangelical: Hymns like "Rock of Ages" and "On Eagle's Wings" draw from this song and related texts. Evangelical preaching often uses the eagle "stirring the nest" as an image of God allowing hardship to strengthen faith.
  • Orthodox: The Song of Moses is the second of the nine biblical odes sung in Orthodox Matins, especially in Lent. James, brother of the Lord, honored today, is credited with the oldest Orthodox liturgy still in occasional use.
  • Live debates today: Feminine images of God, like "the God who gave you birth," are discussed in debates about inclusive language in worship. Another debate: how to read the song's later verses about God's vengeance.

Carry it today: Tell someone one story of how God carried you, so it isn't forgotten.

StoicismWiki ↗

What is Stoicism? A philosophy founded by Zeno of Citium around 300 BC in Athens, named for the painted porch (stoa) where he taught. Its most famous teachers are Epictetus, born into slavery; Seneca, a Roman statesman; and the emperor Marcus Aurelius. The Stoics taught that we can't control events, only our judgments and choices. Their four virtues are wisdom, courage, justice and self-control.

"From my grandfather Verus I learned good morals and the government of my temper. From the reputation and remembrance of my father, modesty and a manly character." (Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 1.1–2, Long translation.) The emperor's private journal opens not with his own ideas but with a long list of the people who formed him: grandfather, father, mother, teachers, friends and the gods. It's a Stoic song of remembering. Moses' song does the same for a whole people: before you look ahead, remember who carried you here.

The Song's archaic Hebrew and poe

The time. The Song of Moses is set at the end of Moses' life, around the 13th century BC. Many scholars date the poem itself to the 11th–8th centuries BC, older than most of Deuteronomy. Titus was written in Paul's name, probably in the late 1st century.

What scholars broadly agree on. The Song's archaic Hebrew and poetic style mark it as one of the oldest texts in the Bible. Its structure resembles a covenant lawsuit: heaven and earth are called as witnesses, God's faithfulness is recounted, and the people's unfaithfulness is charged. Fragments of the song were found among the Dead Sea Scrolls, with some wording that differs from later Hebrew texts. The Hebrew for "gave you birth" in 32:18 uses a verb for labor pains.

What it meant then. For Israel in exile, the song explained their suffering as the result of forgetting God, and promised that God would still vindicate them. For Titus's Cretan churches, living "self-controlled, upright, and godly" lives would show the gospel's power to their neighbors.

The allegorical readings. The fathers read the eagle bearing its young as Christ carrying the soul to heaven, and honey from the rock as the sweetness of grace from Christ crucified.

What's debated or unknown. Scholars debate when the song was composed and whether it predates Moses' story around it. A Dead Sea Scroll reading of 32:8 speaks of "the sons of God" where later texts say "sons of Israel," a difference that has sparked much discussion.
