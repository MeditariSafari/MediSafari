“The coin carries Caesar's face. We carry God's. Give each what bears its image.”
October 18th, 2026The five-minute version

The readings: Two scenes about seeing God and giving God what's due. In Exodus, Moses has just begged God not to abandon the people after the golden calf, and God promises, "My presence will go with you." But Moses wants more: "Please show me your glory." God says no one can see God's face and live, so God tucks Moses into a crack in the rock, covers him with a hand, and lets him see God's back as the glory passes. In Matthew, Jesus' enemies try to trap him: should Jews pay taxes to Rome? Yes makes him a traitor to his people; no makes him a rebel. Jesus asks for a coin. It has the emperor's face on it. "Give therefore to Caesar the things that are Caesar's and to God the things that are God's."

What it means: The coin is stamped with Caesar's image, so it belongs to Caesar. But every person is stamped with God's image (Genesis 1:27), so we belong to God. Jesus isn't dividing life into two equal halves; he's reminding us that the emperor gets a coin, and God gets everything else, including us. And Moses shows what it's like to belong to a God we can't fully see: we glimpse God's goodness passing by, and we see where God has been more clearly than where God is.

Across the traditions: the same truth, in other words.

  • Tao Te Ching: "We look at it, and we do not see it, and we name it 'the Equable.'"
  • Epictetus: if we truly believed "that we are all sprung from God," we would "never have any ignoble or mean thoughts" about ourselves.
  • Bhagavad Gita: Arjuna asks to see Krishna's true form, can't bear it, and begs for the familiar face again (Ch. 11, paraphrased).
  • Islam: the Qur'an says when Moses asked to see God, God told him to look at the mountain, and Moses fell down stunned (7:143, paraphrased).
  • Sikh: Guru Nanak taught that God's light is in every heart, the same light in all (paraphrased).

Carry it today: If you're stamped with God's image, what in your life is still being handed to some "Caesar" that belongs to God?

The theme

  • Whose face is on it: The coin bears Caesar's head and title. Jesus' answer turns the question around: what bears God's image?
  • The cleft of the rock: Moses can't see God's face, but God shelters him, covers him and passes by in goodness.
  • The back of God: Moses sees where God has been. Often we recognize God's presence only looking back.
  • Turned from idols: Paul praises the Thessalonians who "turned to God from idols," choosing whose image they would serve.

Last Sunday the people made a golden calf, an image of their own making. This week Moses asks to see the true God and is shown only goodness passing by, while Jesus points to Caesar's image on a coin. Every reading asks the same thing: whose image will we bow to, and whose image do we bear?

1. My Presence Will Go with You

"My presence will go with you, and I will give you rest."

  • After the Calf: God had threatened to send the people on with only an angel. Moses refuses to go without God himself, and God agrees.
  • Rest: The promise isn't an easy road; it's company on the road. God's presence is the rest.

2. Please Show Me Your Glory

"Please show me your glory." God answers, "I will make all my goodness pass before you and will proclaim before you the name, 'The Lord,'" but "you cannot see my face, for no one shall see me and live."

  • Glory as Goodness: Moses asks for glory, and God gives goodness. God's glory is God's goodness on the move.
  • A Name, Not a Face: God gives Moses the divine name, a relationship to call on, rather than a picture to hold.

3. The Cleft of the Rock

"While my glory passes by I will put you in a cleft of the rock" and "I will cover you with my hand until I have passed by; then I will take away my hand, and you shall see my back, but my face shall not be seen."

  • Sheltered: The God who could overwhelm Moses protects him, with a rock and with a hand. The old hymn "Rock of Ages, cleft for me" draws on this scene.
  • Looking Back: Moses sees God's back. Many people find their deepest sense of God by looking back over the road they've traveled.

4. Whose Head Is This?

"Teacher, we know that you are sincere, and teach the way of God in accordance with truth." But Jesus, "aware of their malice," asks, "Why are you putting me to the test, you hypocrites?" They bring a denarius. "Whose head is this and whose title?"

  • Flattery as a Trap: The questioners praise Jesus' honesty while setting a snare. Their words are true; their hearts aren't.
  • The Coin in Their Pocket: In the Temple, devout Jews avoided images. By producing a coin with Caesar's face, his opponents show they already carry Caesar's image with them.

5. To Caesar and to God

"Give therefore to Caesar the things that are Caesar's and to God the things that are God's." "When they heard this, they were amazed, and they left him and went away." The answer escapes the trap but carries a deeper claim. The coin bears Caesar's image, so give it back; humans bear God's image (Genesis 1:26–27), so give yourselves back to God. The early Christian writer Tertullian made exactly this point: give Caesar his image on the coin, and God his image in you. Moses on the mountain shows what that belonging feels like: we don't get to see God's face, but we live in God's presence, sheltered and passed by in goodness. The Thessalonians show what it looks like: turning from idols, a work of faith, a labor of love. Whose face is stamped on how we spend our money, our time and our loyalty?

For Christians today

"Give to Caesar" has been read in almost every way imaginable, and "the cleft of the rock" has comforted believers for centuries.

  • The oldest readings: Early Christians like Tertullian (around AD 200) read the coin and the image of God together: Caesar gets the coin, God gets the whole person. The church fathers read Moses in the rock as a picture of Christ, the rock in whom we are sheltered to see God's glory (compare 1 Corinthians 10:4), and Gregory of Nyssa read "seeing God's back" as following God forever, always a step behind.
  • Catholic: The Catechism teaches that citizens owe authorities respect, taxes and service, but must refuse when laws contradict God's moral order: "We must obey God rather than men" (§2240–2242, quoting Acts 5:29). Today is also World Mission Sunday.
  • Protestant and evangelical: Luther's "two kingdoms" teaching drew on this passage: God rules through both the state and the gospel, in different ways. Many evangelicals read it as a call to good citizenship, while Anabaptist traditions have stressed that Caesar's claims stop where conscience begins.
  • Orthodox: Orthodox theology loves Exodus 33: Moses in the darkness of God, seeing only God's "back," is the model of the mystic who knows God by love more than by sight. Today the Orthodox also celebrate St. Luke, who tradition says painted the first icon of Mary.
  • Live debates today: How much do Christians owe the state? The passage is cited by those who urge obedience to government, by those who say God's claim comes first, and in debates about taxes, war and civil disobedience. Scholars generally agree that Jesus wasn't laying out a theory of church and state; he was refusing a trap and pointing to God's greater claim.

Carry it today: Look at the face on one coin or bill today, and remember whose image is on you.

Today's readings through a Sikh lens: one light in every heart.

  • God in all: Guru Nanak taught that the one Creator's light shines in every being; the Guru Granth Sahib says God's light is in all, and by that light all are lit (paraphrased). Every face bears the same light.

StoicismWiki ↗

What is Stoicism? A philosophy founded by Zeno of Citium around 300 BC in Athens, named for the painted porch (stoa) where he taught. Its most famous teachers are Epictetus, born into slavery; Seneca, a Roman statesman; and the emperor Marcus Aurelius. The Stoics taught that we can't control events, only our judgments and choices. Their four virtues are wisdom, courage, justice and self-control.

"If a man should be able to assent to this doctrine as he ought, that we are all sprung from God in an especial manner, and that God is the father both of men and of gods, I suppose that he would never have any ignoble or mean thoughts about himself." (Epictetus, Discourses 1.3, Long translation.) Epictetus, a former slave, taught that every person shares in the divine, so no emperor can make anyone small. It's the Stoic version of Jesus' coin: Caesar may own the money, but the person belongs to God. Treat yourself, and everyone you meet today, as a child of God.

Today's readings through Hindu and Jain eyes: Maha Ashtami and the vision of God.

  • The Hindu lens: Today is Maha Ashtami, the eighth and greatest day of Navaratri, honoring Mahagauri, the radiant white form of the Goddess. Many families honor young girls as living images of the Goddess (Kanya Puja), and the Sandhi Puja is performed at the moment Ashtami turns to Navami. In the Bhagavad Gita, Arjuna asks to see Krishna's universal form; when he sees it, he trembles and begs for the familiar face again (ch. 11, paraphrased), Moses' story told in India.
  • The Jain lens: Jains teach that every soul (jiva) is divine by nature, its pure light hidden by karma. Liberation means uncovering the face of the soul itself.

Today is Shukla Ashtami, the eighth day of the bright half (Saptami ends at 8:28 AM India time), with the moon moving from Purva Ashadha into Uttara Ashadha (per Indian calendars; exact Chicago times couldn't be confirmed). Tomorrow is Maha Navami.

What the words mean (all Sanskrit):

  • Mahagauri: mahā, "great," plus gaurī, "white, radiant."
  • Ashtami: "the eighth" (from aṣṭa, "eight," compare Latin octo).
  • Kanya: "girl, maiden."
  • Sandhi: "joining," the meeting point of two days.
  • Uttara Ashadha: "the latter invincible one."

The time. Exodus 33 belongs to the story of Sinai, traditionally dated around the 13th century BC, and was shaped in writing over centuries. The tax debate happened in Jerusalem around AD 30, the week before Jesus' death. Paul wrote to Thessalonica around AD 50.

What scholars broadly agree on. The tax was the Roman tribute or head tax, imposed on Judea since AD 6, when a revolt led by Judas the Galilean opposed it as idolatry. The denarius of Emperor Tiberius bore his portrait and an inscription calling him "son of the divine Augustus," which many Jews found offensive. The Herodians supported the Roman-backed Herod family; the Pharisees resented Roman rule. Their alliance shows how badly they wanted Jesus caught.

What it meant then. For Jews, the face of a ruler claiming divinity was close to an idol. Jesus' question "whose image?" echoes the Bible's language of images and idols: last week's golden calf, the second commandment, and humanity made "in the image of God."

The allegorical readings. The fathers read the cleft of the rock as Christ, and God's "back" as the incarnation: we see God in Christ's humanity, the face of God turned toward us. Augustine compared the coin to the soul: as the coin bears Caesar's image, the soul bears God's, and must be returned to its maker.

What's debated or unknown. Scholars debate whether Jesus' answer endorsed paying the tax, cleverly sidestepped it, or quietly subverted it (if everything belongs to God, what's left for Caesar?). In Exodus, scholars discuss how these verses fit with nearby ones where Moses speaks with God "face to face, as one speaks to a friend" (33:11).
