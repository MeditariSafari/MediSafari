October 11th, 2026The five-minute version

The readings: A king throws a wedding banquet for his son. The guests he invited make excuses: one goes to his farm, another to his business, and some even attack the messengers. So the king sends his servants out into the streets to invite everyone they meet, "bad and good alike," until the hall is full. But when the king comes in, he finds one man not wearing a wedding garment, and has him thrown out. Isaiah describes the same kind of feast: God on the mountain, spreading rich food and fine wine for every nation, wiping away every tear and destroying death forever. Psalm 23 adds the table God sets for us, and Paul says he's learned to be content with plenty or with little.

What it means: God's feast is open to everyone, the respectable and the disreputable, insiders and outsiders. The tragedy in the parable isn't that people were left out; it's that the invited guests were too busy to come. The puzzling garment at the end reminds us that the invitation is free but not cheap: coming to the feast means letting ourselves be clothed in something new. It connects to yesterday's "clothed in Christ." Grace opens the door, and then it changes us.

Across the traditions: the same truth, in other words.

  • Epictetus: behave in life "as at a dinner party": take your share with moderation when the dish comes round.
  • Martin Luther King Jr.: he dreamed of everyone sitting "together at the table of brotherhood."
  • Confucius: with coarse rice and water, "I have still joy in the midst of these things."
  • Islam: the Qur'an's chapter "The Table Spread" tells of Jesus asking God for a feast from heaven.
  • Bahá'í: Bahá'u'lláh asked his followers to share a feast every month, "though only water be served."

Carry it today: What's keeping you too busy to accept an invitation to joy?

The theme

  • Everyone is invited: The king's servants bring in "bad and good alike." God's guest list has no velvet rope.
  • Excuses keep people out: The invited guests aren't wicked; they're busy. A farm, a business. The ordinary things that crowd out joy.
  • Come as you are, but don't stay that way: The wedding garment shows that the feast changes us. We're welcomed as we are and then clothed in something new.
  • A table in the dark: Psalm 23 promises God sets the table "in the sight of my foes." The feast isn't only for later; it's spread for us now, even in hard times.

This week the readings moved from prayer, to asking, to filling the empty house, to who belongs. Today they end where it all leads: a wedding feast, with everyone invited.

1. The Invitations Go Out

In the ancient world, a wealthy host sent two invitations: one in advance, and a second when the feast was ready. Refusing the second was a public insult.

  • Too Busy: "Some ignored the invitation and went away, one to his farm, another to his business." Not evil plans, just ordinary life taking first place.
  • Worse Than Busy: Others seize the servants and kill them. Matthew is thinking of how the prophets, and Jesus himself, were treated.

2. The Streets Fill the Hall

The king tells his servants to "go out into the main roads and invite to the feast whomever you find." They bring in everyone, "bad and good alike."

  • No Qualifications: The guests weren't chosen for their goodness. Matthew's own community, Jews and Gentiles, saints and sinners, saw themselves in that crowd.
  • A Full House: God's desire isn't a small, exclusive party. "The hall was filled with guests."

3. The Wedding Garment

The strangest part: the king finds a man "not dressed in a wedding garment," asks him, "My friend, how is it that you came in here without a wedding garment?", and the man is "reduced to silence." He's bound and thrown out.

  • What Is the Garment?: Christians have read it as faith, as love, as good works, as baptism, or as the righteousness of Christ himself. The common thread: the guest accepted the invitation but refused to be changed by it.
  • Silence: He doesn't offer an excuse. He just doesn't care enough to take part.

4. A Feast for All Peoples

Isaiah's vision is even wider than the parable. On the mountain, God makes a feast "for all peoples," destroys "the veil that veils all peoples," and swallows up death forever.

  • The Messianic Banquet: Jewish tradition came to picture the age to come as a great feast. Jesus draws on that image here and at the Last Supper.
  • Tears Wiped Away: "The Lord God will wipe away the tears from every face." Revelation quotes this line in its last chapters.

5. Content at Any Table

Paul writes from prison that he knows how to live "in humble circumstances" and "with abundance," well fed or hungry. "I have the strength for everything through him who empowers me."

  • Not Self-Help: This famous verse isn't about winning games or achieving goals. It's about being content in any circumstance because Christ is with us.
  • Accept the Invitation: Put the readings together: God has set the table, sent the invitation, and opened the doors to everyone. The only thing that can keep us out is our own refusal, or our coming without letting the feast change us.
For Christians today

The wedding banquet is one of Jesus's most challenging parables, and Philippians 4:13 is one of the most quoted verses in the Bible.

  • The oldest readings: Augustine argued that the wedding garment is love (caritas), since faith and sacraments can be shared by good and bad alike, but only love marks the true guest. Gregory the Great preached the same, and that reading became standard in the West.
  • Catholic: The Mass itself is understood as a foretaste of "the wedding feast of the Lamb" (Revelation 19:9); before Communion the priest says, "Blessed are those called to the supper of the Lamb." The white garment given at baptism is often linked to the wedding garment.
  • Protestant and evangelical: Many Reformers read the wedding garment as the righteousness of Christ, given freely to sinners, not earned by good works. Evangelicals often preach this parable as an evangelistic invitation: the door is open, but you must respond.
  • Orthodox: The Orthodox Church sings a Holy Week hymn based on this parable: "I see your bridal chamber adorned, O my Savior, but I have no garment to enter it." Today the Orthodox also honor the Seventh Ecumenical Council, which defended icons as windows on heaven.
  • Live debates today: Christians debate whether "many are invited but few are chosen" means few will be saved, or is a warning against complacency. Churches also wrestle with who is welcome at the Communion table, and Philippians 4:13 is regularly debated when it's used as a motto for athletic or financial success.

Carry it today: Notice the next invitation you're tempted to decline, to a meal, a conversation, a moment of prayer, and say yes.

Buddhist TraditionWiki ↗

Today's readings through a Buddhist lens: the uposatha and the offering of food.

  • What's an uposatha day? The uposatha (Pali, from Sanskrit upavasatha, "fast day," from upa, "near," plus vas, "to dwell") is the Buddhist observance day, kept on the new, full and quarter moons. Lay people visit monasteries, take the Eight Precepts (including not eating after noon), meditate and listen to teachings. On new and full moons, monks gather to recite the Patimokkha, their rule of discipline.
  • The meal as practice: Monks eat what is offered to them, from rich and poor alike, and reflect before eating that the food is "not for fun, not for pleasure," but to sustain the body for practice. The Buddha's last meal was offered by Cunda, a humble metalworker, and the Buddha made sure Cunda would not be blamed for his death.

Today is a new-moon uposatha (per the Wat Metta calendar). The rains retreat ends with Pavāraṇā on October 26.

StoicismWiki ↗

What is Stoicism? A Greek and Roman philosophy founded around 300 BC by Zeno of Citium, who taught under the painted porch (stoa) in Athens. Its best-known voices are Epictetus, Seneca and Marcus Aurelius. Stoics divide life into what is up to us (our judgments, choices and actions) and what isn't (other people, fortune, reputation), and put their effort into the first. Their four cardinal virtues are wisdom, courage, justice and self-control.

"Remember that you must behave in life as at a dinner party. Is anything brought around to you? Put out your hand and take your share with moderation." (Epictetus, Enchiridion 15, Higginson translation.) Epictetus goes on: if a dish passes you by, don't grab for it; if it hasn't come yet, wait patiently. Life is a banquet you didn't plan. Paul calls the same attitude contentment: "I have learned the secret of being well fed and of going hungry."

Today's readings through Hindu and Jain eyes: Navaratri and the sacred meal.

  • The Hindu lens: Today is the first day of Sharad Navaratri, nine nights honoring the Goddess in her nine forms. Day one honors Shailaputri, "daughter of the mountain," and many homes set up the kalash (a sacred water pot) at Ghatasthapana. Many Hindus also see food itself as holy: prasad, food first offered to God and then shared with all, is given to everyone who comes, rich or poor. It's a feast on the mountain, in Isaiah's language.
  • The Jain lens: Jains practice careful eating as a spiritual discipline, avoiding harm to living beings and often not eating after sunset. Jain tradition also honors giving food to monks and nuns, and to the hungry, as one of the highest forms of charity (ahara dana, "the gift of food").

Today is Shukla Pratipada, the first day of the bright half, with the moon in Chitra (per Indian calendars; exact Chicago times couldn't be confirmed). Sunday belongs to Surya, the sun.

What the words mean (all Sanskrit):

  • Pratipada: "the first step," the first lunar day of each half-month.
  • Shukla: "bright, white": the waxing half of the month.
  • Shailaputri: śaila, "mountain," plus putrī, "daughter."
  • Prasad: prasāda, "grace, favor, clearness": food blessed by being offered to God.
  • Dana: dāna, "giving" (from the root dā, "to give," the same root as Latin donum, "gift," and English "donate").

The time. Jesus told this parable in Jerusalem during his last week, around AD 30. Matthew wrote his Gospel around AD 80–90, after the Romans had destroyed Jerusalem in AD 70. Isaiah 24–27 was probably written in the Persian period, after the return from exile in Babylon, though scholars debate the date. Paul wrote Philippians from prison, probably in Rome, Ephesus or Caesarea.

What scholars broadly agree on. Most scholars think Jesus told a banquet parable that Matthew and Luke each shaped differently: Luke's version (Luke 14:15–24) is simpler, while Matthew adds the burned city and the wedding garment. Many think "sent his troops, destroyed those murderers, and burned their city" (verse 7) reflects the destruction of Jerusalem in AD 70, which Matthew's readers had lived through. Double invitations to feasts were a real custom among the elite of the time.

What it meant then. For Matthew's community, the parable explained why many religious leaders had rejected Jesus while outsiders, Gentiles and sinners, had come in. It also warned the new insiders not to be complacent: being in the hall wasn't the same as being ready. Isaiah's feast offered hope to a small, battered people that God's salvation would one day reach every nation and defeat death itself.

The allegorical readings. The church fathers read the king as God, the son as Christ, the servants as the prophets and apostles, and the wedding as the union of Christ and the Church. The wedding garment was read as love (Augustine, Gregory the Great), as baptismal grace, or as good works. The guests from the streets were seen as the Gentiles. These are interpretations, not the parable's plain meaning.

What's debated or unknown. Scholars debate whether the wedding garment story was a separate parable that Matthew attached. There's no clear evidence that ancient hosts handed out festive robes to guests, so the garment may be symbolic rather than a real custom. Scholars also debate the meaning of "many are invited, but few are chosen," whether it describes numbers or simply contrasts invitation with response.
