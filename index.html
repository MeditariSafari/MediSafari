October 29th, 2026The five-minute version

The readings: "My servant Moses is dead." With those words, the book of Joshua begins a new chapter for Israel. For forty years Moses had led them; now Joshua, his assistant, must take them across the Jordan into a land none of them has seen. God's words to him are simple: "I will not fail you or forsake you. Be strong and courageous," "for the Lord your God is with you wherever you go." Psalm 107 thanks God for turning deserts into pools of water. And Paul challenges teachers who preach the law but don't live it: real faith is "a matter of the heart."

What it means: Every generation has its "Moses is dead" moment: a leader gone, a familiar world ended, a river to cross. God doesn't promise Joshua the crossing will be easy. He promises presence: I'll be with you as I was with Moses. Courage isn't the absence of fear; it's moving forward because you're not alone.

Across the traditions: the same truth, in other words.

  • Epictetus: "Men are disturbed, not by things, but by the principles and notions which they form concerning things." (Enchiridion 5.)
  • Bhagavad Gita: Krishna tells the frightened Arjuna to cast off faintheartedness and stand up (2:3, paraphrased).
  • Buddha: Fearlessness (abhaya) is shown in the Buddha's raised open hand, a gesture of protection and courage.
  • Islam: The Qur'an says, do not lose heart or grieve, for you will prevail if you are believers (3:139, paraphrased).
  • Tao Te Ching: Through compassion, one can be brave (Ch. 67, paraphrased).

Carry it today: What river are you standing in front of, and what would courage look like?

The theme

  • Moses is dead: The leader is gone. Someone else must step up.
  • Not alone: "I will not fail you or forsake you."
  • Courage: "Be strong and courageous" is repeated three times in this chapter.
  • The heart: Paul says the real mark of faith is inward, not outward.

For weeks the RCL followed Moses. Now the story moves to Joshua, and Sunday's readings will take the people through the Jordan on dry ground.

1. My Servant Moses Is Dead

"My servant Moses is dead. Now proceed to cross the Jordan, you and all this people."

  • An Ending and a Beginning: God names the grief, then points forward.
  • "Servant": Moses' highest title is servant of the Lord. Joshua will carry the same title at his death.

2. I Will Not Fail You

"As I was with Moses, so I will be with you; I will not fail you or forsake you."

  • Same God: The leader changes; God doesn't.
  • Hebrews' Echo: The New Testament repeats this promise to all believers: "I will never leave you or forsake you" (Hebrews 13:5, paraphrased).

3. Be Strong and Courageous

"Be strong and courageous," "for the Lord your God is with you wherever you go."

  • Three Times: God says it twice, and the people say it a third time (Joshua 1:6, 9, 18).
  • Courage Rooted in Presence: The reason to be brave isn't Joshua's strength but God's company.

4. Deserts into Pools

"Some wandered in desert wastes, finding no way to an inhabited town." "He turns a desert into pools of water, a parched land into springs of water."

  • The Wilderness Behind: Israel's forty years of wandering are almost over.
  • Thanksgiving: Psalm 107 is for anyone God has brought through.

5. A Matter of the Heart

"You, then, who teach others, will you not teach yourself?" Paul says true belonging to God "is a matter of the heart, by the Spirit, not the written code." Joshua was told to meditate on God's law "day and night" and not let it depart from his mouth (Joshua 1:8, paraphrased): not as an outward rulebook but as something lived from the inside. Paul makes the same point to people who were proud of knowing the law. The courage God asks of Joshua isn't bravado; it comes from a heart rooted in God. That's what carries us across the rivers we can't avoid.

For Christians today

"Be strong and courageous" is one of the most quoted verses in Christian life, from soldiers to graduates to people facing illness.

  • The oldest readings: Joshua's name in Greek is "Jesus." The fathers saw Joshua leading the people into the land, where Moses could not, as a picture of Jesus leading believers into God's kingdom.
  • Catholic: Today's Catholic first reading, the armor of God (Ephesians 6), and the Gospel of Jesus facing Herod's threat both speak of courage in the face of danger.
  • Protestant and evangelical: Joshua 1:9 is a favorite memory verse and often given to people starting something new. Evangelical teaching stresses meditating on scripture "day and night," as Joshua was told.
  • Orthodox: The Orthodox read Joshua's crossing of the Jordan as a type of baptism, celebrated at Theophany (January 6), when Jesus was baptized in the same river.
  • Live debates today: The conquest stories in Joshua raise hard questions about violence in scripture; many churches read them carefully, alongside the archaeological evidence.

Carry it today: Tell someone facing something new: you're not alone in this.

What is Stoicism? A philosophy founded by Zeno of Citium around 300 BC in Athens, named for the painted porch (stoa) where he taught. Its most famous teachers are Epictetus, born into slavery; Seneca, a Roman statesman; and the emperor Marcus Aurelius. The Stoics taught that we can't control events, only our judgments and choices. Their four virtues are wisdom, courage, justice and self-control.

"Men are disturbed, not by things, but by the principles and notions which they form concerning things. Death, for instance, is not terrible, else it would have appeared so to Socrates. But the terror consists in our notion of death that it is terrible." (Epictetus, Enchiridion 5, Carter translation.) For Epictetus, courage begins in how we see things. The river isn't the problem; our fear of it is. Joshua's courage comes from a different source, the promise of God's presence, but both teach that fear doesn't get the final word on what we do.

Today's readings through Hindu and Jain eyes: Karva Chauth.

  • The Hindu lens: Today is Karva Chauth, when many married Hindu women fast from sunrise until they see the moon, praying for their husbands' long life. It's a day of faithfulness and endurance.   • The Jain lens: Jains honor the Tirthankaras, "ford-makers," who crossed the river of rebirth and made a crossing for others, much like Joshua leading Israel over the Jordan.

Today is Krishna Chaturthi (from 1:06 AM India time), with the moon moving from Rohini into Mrigashira (per Indian calendars; exact Chicago times couldn't be confirmed).

What the words mean (all Sanskrit or Hindi):

  • Karva Chauth: karva, "clay pot," plus chauth, "fourth" (the fourth day of the waning moon).
  • Tirthankara: "ford-maker," one who makes a crossing.
  • Mrigashira: "the deer's head."

The time. Joshua is set around the late 13th century BC. Most scholars think the book took shape centuries later, as part of the "Deuteronomistic history" (Deuteronomy through Kings), in the 7th–6th centuries BC. Romans was written by Paul around AD 57.

What scholars broadly agree on. Archaeology doesn't support a rapid conquest of Canaan as Joshua describes; many scholars think Israel emerged more gradually from within Canaan. Joshua 1 is written in Deuteronomy's style, with its emphasis on courage, obedience and the law.

What it meant then. For readers in exile, Joshua 1 was a promise: God would bring them home again across the river, if they held to the law. For Paul's Roman readers, Romans 2 leveled the ground between Jews and Gentiles: everyone needs grace.

The allegorical readings. Origen wrote a series of homilies on Joshua, reading the crossing of the Jordan as baptism and the battles as the soul's struggle against vice.

What's debated or unknown. How much history lies behind the conquest stories is strongly debated. How to read Romans 2's "doers of the law" alongside Paul's teaching on faith is a long-running discussion.
