October 27th, 2026The five-minute version

The readings: When Moses came down from Mount Sinai with the new tablets of the law, his face was shining, and he didn't even know it. He'd been talking with God. The people were afraid to come near, so he wore a veil, taking it off only when he went in to speak with God again. James, centuries later, asks a sharp question: what good is faith that doesn't do anything? If a brother or sister is hungry and you say "Go in peace; keep warm and eat your fill" but give them nothing, what good is that? Faith without works is dead. "Show me your faith apart from works, and I by my works will show you faith."

What it means: Moses' shining face is what happens when someone spends time with God: it shows, even when they don't know it. James says the same about faith: if it's real, it shows in how we treat people. Neither one is something you can fake or announce. It just comes through.

Across the traditions: the same truth, in other words.

  • Epictetus: "Sheep don't throw up the grass to show the shepherds how much they have eaten; but, inwardly digesting their food, they outwardly produce wool and milk." (Enchiridion 46.)
  • Pirkei Avot: Shammai taught, say little and do much (Avot 1:15, paraphrased).
  • Confucius: He said he used to listen to people's words and trust their deeds; now he listens to their words and watches their deeds (Analects 5.10, paraphrased).
  • Bhagavad Gita: Krishna says no one can remain without action even for a moment, and calls Arjuna to act (3:5, 3:8, paraphrased).
  • Islam: The Qur'an again and again pairs "those who believe and do good deeds" (e.g., 103:3, paraphrased).

The theme

  • The shining face: Time with God leaves a mark that others see before we do.
  • The veil: Moses covers the glory so the people can come near.
  • Faith that works: James won't separate believing from doing.
  • Show me: Real faith is shown, not claimed.

Yesterday the RCL remembered Aaron's death; today it steps back to Moses at his brightest, the shining face from Sinai. Tomorrow the readings move on.

1. Moses Did Not Know

"Moses did not know that the skin of his face shone because he had been talking with God."

  • Unaware: The most striking detail: Moses didn't know. Holiness doesn't notice itself.
  • Horns of Light: The Hebrew word for "shone" (qaran) is related to "horn"; Jerome's Latin translated it "horned," which is why Michelangelo's Moses has horns.

2. Afraid to Come Near

"When Aaron and all the Israelites saw Moses, the skin of his face was shining, and they were afraid to come near him."

  • Glory Is Frightening: People who've been close to God can unsettle the rest of us.
  • Moses Calls Them: Moses calls them back and speaks with them anyway. Glory isn't a wall.

3. The Veil

"When Moses had finished speaking with them, he put a veil on his face, but whenever Moses went in before the Lord to speak with him, he would take the veil off."

  • Unveiled Before God: Before God, Moses hides nothing.
  • Paul's Reading: Paul says that in Christ "all of us, with unveiled faces," reflect the Lord's glory and are being transformed (2 Corinthians 3:18, paraphrased).

4. Faith Without Works

"What good is it, my brothers and sisters, if someone claims to have faith but does not have works?" "So faith by itself, if it has no works, is dead."

  • Kind Words, Empty Hands: James imagines blessing a hungry person and sending them away. "What is the good of that?"
  • Dead, Not Weak: James doesn't say faith without works is weak; he says it's dead.

5. I by My Works Will Show You Faith

"Show me your faith apart from works, and I by my works will show you faith." James' challenge is Moses' face in another key. Faith isn't a private opinion you can hold without anyone noticing; it's a life that shines. James names Abraham, who offered Isaac, and Rahab, the outsider who hid Israel's spies, as people whose faith became visible in what they risked. "For just as the body without the spirit is dead, so faith without works is also dead." The Episcopal Gospel today says the same: "Blessed rather are those who hear the word of God and obey it!" And like Moses, the people whose faith shines most often don't know it.

For Christians today

James 2 has been at the center of one of Christianity's biggest arguments.

  • The oldest readings: Augustine harmonized Paul and James: Paul speaks of works before faith, which can't save; James of works after faith, which show it's alive. Early Christians read Moses' shining face as a foretaste of Christ's Transfiguration.
  • Catholic: The Council of Trent (1547) cited James 2:24 in teaching that faith and works together are part of justification. Catholic social teaching puts James' hungry neighbor at its center.
  • Protestant and evangelical: Luther called James "an epistle of straw" because it seemed to contradict Paul's "justified by faith." Most Protestants now read James as describing the fruit of true faith: we're saved by faith alone, but saving faith is never alone.
  • Orthodox: The Orthodox see Moses' shining face as a sign of theosis, being filled with God's uncreated light, the same light seen at the Transfiguration on Mount Tabor.
  • Live debates today: The 1999 Joint Declaration on the Doctrine of Justification, signed by Lutherans and Catholics (later joined by Methodists, Anglicans and Reformed), said the old dispute over faith and works no longer divides them.

StoicismWiki ↗

What is Stoicism? A philosophy founded by Zeno of Citium around 300 BC in Athens, named for the painted porch (stoa) where he taught. Its most famous teachers are Epictetus, born into slavery; Seneca, a Roman statesman; and the emperor Marcus Aurelius. The Stoics taught that we can't control events, only our judgments and choices. Their four virtues are wisdom, courage, justice and self-control.

"Never call yourself a philosopher, nor talk a great deal among the unlearned about theorems, but act conformably to them. Thus, at an entertainment, don't talk how persons ought to eat, but eat as you ought... For sheep don't throw up the grass to show the shepherds how much they have eaten; but, inwardly digesting their food, they outwardly produce wool and milk." (Epictetus, Enchiridion 46, Carter translation.) Epictetus wanted his students to be known not for what they could explain but for how they lived. Sheep don't show off the grass; they give wool and milk. It's James' "I by my works will show you faith," and Moses' shining face: what you take in, digested quietly, shows up in what you give.

The time. Exodus sets Moses' shining face at Sinai, traditionally the 13th century BC; most scholars date the written account to the 7th–5th centuries BC. The Letter of James is dated between the 40s and 90s AD.

What scholars broadly agree on. The shining-face story belongs to the priestly traditions of Exodus and highlights Moses as unique mediator between God and Israel. James draws on Jewish wisdom and Jesus' teaching, especially the Sermon on the Mount, and was likely written for poor Jewish-Christian communities.

What it meant then. For Israel, the shining face confirmed that the renewed covenant, after the golden calf, came truly from God. For James' readers, his challenge was concrete: churches that honored the rich and ignored the poor had a faith that was dead.

The allegorical readings. Paul read Moses' veil as a sign that the old covenant's glory was fading and is fully revealed in Christ (2 Corinthians 3). Gregory of Nyssa saw the shining face as what happens to a soul that keeps climbing toward God.

What's debated or unknown. Whether James was responding to Paul, or to people misreading Paul, is still debated. The meaning of the Hebrew qaran, "shone" or "was horned," has puzzled translators for centuries.
