October 12th, 2026The five-minute version

The readings: More and more people are crowding around Jesus, and some want proof: a sign from heaven. Jesus says no. The only sign they'll get is "the sign of Jonah." The people of Nineveh, Israel's enemies, changed their lives just from hearing Jonah preach. The Queen of Sheba traveled across the world to hear Solomon. "And there is something greater than Jonah here," and still people want more proof. In Galatians, Paul tells the story of Abraham's two sons, one born to an enslaved woman and one to a free woman, and says it's a picture of two ways to live: as slaves or as free children. "For freedom Christ set us free; so stand firm."

What it means: We often want God to prove himself before we change. Jesus says the evidence is already in front of us. The outsiders in his story, foreigners and enemies, responded to much less. Paul adds that the freedom we're waiting for has already been given. The danger isn't that God hasn't acted; it's that we go back to our old chains out of habit or fear. This week starts with a simple challenge: stop asking for a sign, and start living like someone who's free.

Across the traditions: the same truth, in other words.

  • Tao Te Ching: "Without going outside his door, one understands (all that takes place) under the sky."
  • Marcus Aurelius: "Look within. Within is the fountain of good."
  • Epictetus: "He is free who lives as he wishes to live."
  • Islam: the Qur'an says the people of Jonah's city were the one town that believed in time and was spared (10:98).
  • Nelson Mandela: to be free is not merely to cast off one's chains.

Carry it today: What sign are you waiting for before you change something you already know you should?

The theme

  • No sign on demand: Jesus won't perform for an audience that wants proof without change. Faith doesn't begin with a spectacle.
  • The outsiders got it: Nineveh and the Queen of Sheba, foreigners both, responded to far less. Often the people we least expect are the most open.
  • Free, not just forgiven: Paul says Christ didn't just pardon us; he set us free. We're children of the free woman, not the slave.
  • Stand firm: Freedom can be lost by drifting back. The question isn't whether we've been freed, but whether we'll stay free.

Last week ended at the wedding feast. This week begins with a crowd that won't come in without proof, and with Paul's call to stand in the freedom we've already been given.

1. A Generation That Wants a Sign

The crowds are growing, but Jesus is not impressed by numbers. "This generation is an evil generation; it seeks a sign."

  • Proof Before Commitment: Asking for a sign can be a way of keeping God at arm's length: show me first, and then maybe I'll change.
  • Already Given: Jesus has just healed, taught and driven out demons. The signs are everywhere. What's missing is the willingness to respond.

2. The Sign of Jonah

In Luke, the sign of Jonah is Jonah himself: a reluctant prophet who walked into the enemy city of Nineveh with a five-word message (in Hebrew), and the whole city repented, from the king to the cattle.

  • A Sign That Is a Person: The sign isn't a miracle; it's a messenger. Jesus is the greater Jonah, and his presence is the sign.
  • Matthew Adds More: In Matthew's version (12:40), Jonah's three days in the belly of the fish point to Jesus's three days in the tomb. Luke keeps it simpler: listen to the one who's here.

3. Queens and Ninevites

Jesus holds up two outsiders as examples. "The queen of the south will rise at the judgment with the men of this generation and she will condemn them." "At the judgment the men of Nineveh will arise with this generation and condemn it."

  • The Long Journey: The Queen of Sheba came "from the ends of the earth" just to hear wisdom. Many of Jesus's hearers wouldn't cross the street.
  • The Enemies Repented: Nineveh was the capital of Assyria, which destroyed the northern kingdom of Israel. Even they turned to God.

4. Two Mothers, Two Ways of Life

Paul tells the story of Abraham's two sons. Ishmael was born to Hagar, an enslaved Egyptian woman, through human planning; Isaac was born to Sarah, the free woman, through God's promise.

  • An Allegory, Paul Says: Paul openly calls this an allegory: Hagar stands for life under the Law, "bearing children for slavery"; Sarah stands for "the Jerusalem above," which "is freeborn, and she is our mother."
  • Children of the Promise: Believers, Paul says, are "children not of the slave woman but of the freeborn woman." We are heirs, not servants.

5. Stand Firm in Freedom

"For freedom Christ set us free; so stand firm and do not submit again to the yoke of slavery." This is Paul's rallying cry. Freedom isn't only something given; it has to be kept. The Galatians were about to trade their freedom for a new set of rules. We do it too: back to old fears, old habits, old ways of proving ourselves. Jesus says we don't need another sign. Paul says we don't need another yoke. What we need is to stand up in what we've already been given.

For Christians today

Galatians 5:1 has been a rallying cry for Christian freedom movements for centuries, and "the sign of Jonah" is one of Jesus's most debated sayings.

  • The oldest readings: Early Christians often painted Jonah in the catacombs of Rome, more than almost any other Old Testament figure, as a sign of the resurrection. Augustine read Paul's two mothers as two cities: the earthly city and the City of God.
  • Catholic: The Catechism links the sign of Jonah to Christ's death and resurrection (§994) and teaches that true freedom is not doing whatever we want but the power to choose the good, which is perfected by grace (§1731–1733).
  • Protestant and evangelical: Luther's 1520 treatise On the Freedom of a Christian begins with a famous paradox: "A Christian is a perfectly free lord of all, subject to none. A Christian is a perfectly dutiful servant of all, subject to all." Galatians 5:1 was central to it.
  • Orthodox: The Orthodox Church reads the whole Book of Jonah at the Vesperal Liturgy of Holy Saturday, as a sign of Christ's descent among the dead and his rising. Freedom, in Orthodox teaching, is the restored ability to love God freely, which sin had bound.
  • Live debates today: Galatians 4 has a painful history: Christians used the Hagar and Sarah allegory to cast Jewish people as "slaves," fueling contempt. Many churches today reject that reading. Others, including womanist theologians like Delores Williams, read Hagar herself as an enslaved woman God saw and cared for (Genesis 16:13, "the God who sees me"). On Indigenous Peoples' Day, many churches also reflect on the 1493 papal bulls behind the "Doctrine of Discovery," which the Vatican formally repudiated in 2023.

StoicismWiki ↗

What is Stoicism? A philosophy founded by Zeno of Citium around 300 BC in Athens, named for the painted porch (stoa) where he taught. Its most famous teachers are Epictetus, born into slavery; Seneca, a Roman statesman; and the emperor Marcus Aurelius. The Stoics taught that we can't control events, only our judgments and choices, and that true freedom lies there. Their four virtues are wisdom, courage, justice and self-control.

"He is free who lives as he wishes to live; who is neither subject to compulsion nor to hindrance, nor to force; whose movements to action are not impeded, whose desires attain their purpose, and who does not fall into that which he would avoid." (Epictetus, Discourses 4.1, "About Freedom," Long translation.) Epictetus, who had been enslaved, argued that the only way to be truly free is to want only what's in our power: our own choices. No one can enslave a will that doesn't cling to what others control. Paul says Christ has set us free; Epictetus would add, then don't hand your freedom back to fear.

Today's readings through Hindu and Jain eyes: the second night of Navaratri and moksha.

  • The Hindu lens: Today is the second day of Sharad Navaratri, which honors Brahmacharini, the Goddess as a young ascetic, walking barefoot and holding prayer beads and a water pot. She stands for steady discipline, standing firm. The ultimate goal in Hindu thought is moksha, "release" from the cycle of rebirth, the freedom of the soul.
  • The Jain lens: For Jains, the soul is naturally free and all-knowing, but bound by karma. Liberation (moksha) is the soul returning to its true nature. Like Paul's "do not submit again," Jain practice is about not taking on new bonds.

Today is Shukla Dwitiya (also called Bij), the second day of the bright half, with the moon in Swati (per Indian calendars; exact Chicago times couldn't be confirmed). Monday belongs to Shiva.

What the words mean (all Sanskrit):

  • Brahmacharini: brahma, "the sacred, the divine," plus chārinī, "she who walks": "she who walks the sacred path."
  • Moksha: from muc, "to free, release": liberation.
  • Dwitiya: "the second" (from dvi, "two," compare Latin duo).
  • Swati: the nakshatra of the star Arcturus, often read as "independent" or "self-going," fitting for a day about freedom (the etymology is debated).

The time. Jesus's ministry was around AD 28–30; Luke wrote around AD 80–90 and Paul wrote Galatians around AD 50–55. The stories they point back to are much older: Abraham's family in Genesis is set in the second millennium BC, Solomon reigned around 970–930 BC, and Nineveh, the capital of the Assyrian Empire, fell to the Babylonians and Medes in 612 BC.

What scholars broadly agree on. Most scholars think the Book of Jonah was written after the Babylonian exile, in the 5th or 4th century BC, as a story about God's mercy reaching even Israel's enemies, rather than as a historical record. The "sign of Jonah" saying appears in both Matthew and Luke, so it likely comes from a shared source of Jesus's sayings; Luke's version, where Jonah's preaching is the sign, is often thought closer to the original. Galatians 4:24 is one of the only places in the New Testament where the Greek word for "allegory" appears.

What it meant then. For Jesus's hearers, Nineveh and the Queen of Sheba were famous foreigners, and holding them up as examples was a sharp rebuke: pagans responded to less than you've been given. For Paul, the Hagar and Sarah story answered teachers who claimed to be the true children of Abraham because they kept the Law. Paul turned the argument around: the free children are those who live by the promise, by faith.

The allegorical readings. The church fathers read Jonah's three days in the fish as Christ in the tomb, and Jonah cast into the sea to calm the storm as Christ giving his life to save others. Augustine read Hagar and Sarah as the earthly city and the heavenly city. Some medieval writers read the Queen of Sheba as the Church coming from the nations to Christ. These are interpretations, not the passage's plain meaning.

What's debated or unknown. Scholars debate whether Jesus meant Jonah's preaching (as in Luke) or Jonah's rescue from the fish (as in Matthew) as the sign. Paul's allegory is unusual: in Genesis, Hagar's son Ishmael is also blessed by God, and Paul's link between Hagar and Mount Sinai "in Arabia" puzzles interpreters. The Queen of Sheba's kingdom was probably in southwest Arabia (modern Yemen), though Ethiopian tradition places it in Ethiopia.
