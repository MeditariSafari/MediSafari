October 15th, 2026The five-minute version

The readings: Jesus's dinner at the Pharisee's house ends badly. He tells the religious leaders that they build monuments to the prophets their ancestors killed, honoring dead prophets while ignoring living ones. And he says the scholars of the law have "taken away the key of knowledge": they don't go in themselves, and they block the people who are trying to. When Jesus leaves, they start plotting against him. In the first reading, Paul opens his letter to the Ephesians with a burst of praise: God chose us "before the foundation of the world" and has "blessed us in Christ with every spiritual blessing."

What it means: The key of knowledge is meant to open doors, not lock them. Religious people can turn faith into a gatekeeping job: deciding who's in, making it harder for others. Jesus says that's the opposite of what God wants. Paul shows what God wants instead: everyone welcomed, chosen, blessed, all things gathered together in Christ. Today's saint, Teresa of Ávila, spent her life opening the door to prayer for others, even when church authorities tried to shut it.

Across the traditions: the same truth, in other words.

  • Kahlil Gibran: "No man can reveal to you aught but that which already lies half asleep in the dawning of your knowledge."
  • Buddha: follow the one "who tells you where true treasures are to be found."
  • Marcus Aurelius: "If any man is able to convince me and show me that I do not think or act right, I will gladly change."
  • Teresa of Ávila: the soul is a castle, and the door into it is prayer.
  • Tao Te Ching: "the skilful closer needs no bolts or bars."

Carry it today: Is there a door you're keeping closed to someone who's trying to get in?

The theme

  • Monuments to dead prophets: It's easy to honor a prophet once they're safely gone. The hard part is listening to the living ones.
  • The key of knowledge: Faith is a key meant to open doors for others. Jesus grieves when it's used to lock them.
  • The plot begins: Telling the truth at the dinner table costs Jesus. From here, his enemies are watching.
  • Chosen before the world began: Paul's answer to gatekeeping: God chose us, blessed us, and plans to gather all things together. Nobody earns their way in.

All week Jesus has been at the same dinner table: no sign for the crowd, the inside of the cup, herbs and fruit. Today it ends with a key, and with Paul's reminder that God threw the door open before the world began.

1. Monuments to the Prophets

"Woe to you! You build the memorials of the prophets whom your ancestors killed."

  • Safe Prophets: A prophet in a tomb is easy to honor; he can't challenge you anymore.
  • The Same Spirit: "They killed them and you do the building." Jesus says building the tomb finishes the job: the message stays buried.

2. From Abel to Zechariah

Jesus says this generation will answer "from the blood of Abel to the blood of Zechariah" (11:51).

  • First to Last: Abel is the first murder in Genesis; Zechariah, stoned in the Temple court (2 Chronicles 24:20–22), is the last in the Hebrew Bible's order of books. Jesus covers the whole Bible, A to Z.
  • Jesus Next: The prophets' story is about to become his own story.

3. The Key of Knowledge

"Woe to you, scholars of the law! You have taken away the key of knowledge. You yourselves did not enter and you stopped those trying to enter."

  • Meant to Open: Knowledge of God's ways is a key, given so that people can come in.
  • Locked Out, Locked In: The experts neither went in nor let anyone else in. Jesus's anger is for the people standing outside.

4. The Plot Begins

"When he left, the scribes and Pharisees began to act with hostility toward him and to interrogate him about many things, for they were plotting to catch him at something he might say."

  • The Cost of Truth: Jesus's honesty at the table turns his hosts into enemies.
  • Watching His Words: From now on in Luke, they're listening for something to use against him.

5. Chosen Before the Foundation of the World

Ephesians opens like a song: "Blessed be the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, who has blessed us in Christ with every spiritual blessing in the heavens, as he chose us in him, before the foundation of the world, to be holy and without blemish before him." No one gets in by being let through the gate by an expert. God chose us before we did anything, and his plan is "to sum up all things in Christ, in heaven and on earth." That's the answer to every locked door in today's Gospel. The key is grace, and it was given to be shared.

For Christians today

St. Teresa of Ávila is one of the great teachers of prayer, and Ephesians 1 is one of the great texts on God's choosing.

  • The oldest readings: Early writers like Origen read "the key of knowledge" as the key to understanding Scripture: the experts had the key but used it to keep others out. Irenaeus loved Ephesians 1:10, "to sum up all things in Christ," and built his theology of "recapitulation" on it: Christ gathers up and renews all of human history.
  • Catholic: Teresa of Ávila, named a Doctor of the Church in 1970, taught that prayer is simply a close sharing between friends, time spent with the One we know loves us (paraphrased). Her Interior Castle pictures the soul as a castle of many rooms, with God at the center. The Catechism quotes her definition of prayer (§2709).
  • Protestant and evangelical: Ephesians 1 is central in Reformed teaching on election: God's choosing comes before anything we do. Arminian and Wesleyan Christians read the same passage as God choosing to save everyone who is "in Christ," a group anyone can join by faith. Both agree that salvation starts with God.
  • Orthodox: Orthodox teaching speaks of God's eternal plan to unite all creation with himself (theosis), and reads Ephesians 1:10 as the goal of the whole universe. Prayer of the heart, like Teresa's, has a parallel in the Jesus Prayer of the Orthodox monastic tradition.
  • Live debates today: Teresa had Jewish ancestry (her grandfather was a converso) and faced suspicion from the Spanish Inquisition, which examined her autobiography. Today, churches still wrestle with "the key of knowledge": who gets to teach, who gets to interpret Scripture, and how women's voices like Teresa's are heard.

Carry it today: Pray Teresa's way for five minutes today: simply talk with God as a friend.

What is Stoicism? A philosophy founded by Zeno of Citium around 300 BC in Athens, named for the painted porch (stoa) where he taught. Its most famous teachers are Epictetus, born into slavery; Seneca, a Roman statesman; and the emperor Marcus Aurelius. The Stoics taught that we can't control events, only our judgments and choices. Their four virtues are wisdom, courage, justice and self-control.

"If any man is able to convince me and show me that I do not think or act right, I will gladly change." (Marcus Aurelius, Meditations Book 6, Long translation.) Marcus goes on to say that he seeks the truth, which never harmed anyone; the person who is harmed is the one who stays in error and ignorance. Here is the most powerful man in the world inviting correction. The scholars of the law did the opposite: when Jesus showed them they were wrong, they started plotting. The Stoic way, and the gospel way, is to let the truth in, even when it stings.

Today's readings through Hindu and Jain eyes: Skandamata and the teacher.

  • The Hindu lens: Today is the fifth day of Sharad Navaratri, which honors Skandamata, "mother of Skanda," shown holding her infant son, the warrior god Skanda (Kartikeya), on her lap. She is the mother who protects and teaches. Thursday belongs to Brihaspati (Jupiter), the teacher of the gods. The guru, in Hindu thought, is the one who removes darkness, the opposite of a gatekeeper.
  • The Jain lens: Jains honor the Tirthankaras, "ford-makers," teachers who found a crossing over the river of rebirth and then showed others the way across. A Tirthankara doesn't guard the ford; they build it for everyone.

Today is Shukla Panchami, the fifth day of the bright half (Chaturthi ends at 1:13 AM India time), with the moon moving from Anuradha into Jyeshtha (per Indian calendars; exact Chicago times couldn't be confirmed).

What the words mean (all Sanskrit):

  • Skandamata: Skanda, the warrior god, plus mātā, "mother."
  • Guru: literally "heavy, weighty"; traditionally explained as gu, "darkness," and ru, "remover."
  • Tirthankara: tīrtha, "ford, crossing place," plus kara, "maker": "ford-maker."
  • Panchami: "the fifth" (from pañca, "five," compare Greek pente).
  • Jyeshtha: "the eldest, the chief," the nakshatra of the star Antares.

Today's readings through a Buddhist lens: no closed fist.

The time. Jesus's ministry was around AD 28–30; Luke wrote around AD 80–90. Ephesians was written either by Paul from prison around AD 60–62 or by a follower around AD 80–90. Ephesus, home to the great Temple of Artemis, was one of the largest cities in the Roman Empire.

What scholars broadly agree on. Monumental tombs for prophets and righteous people were being built around Jerusalem in Jesus's time; tombs traditionally linked to the prophets can still be seen in the Kidron Valley. Luke's "wisdom of God said" quotes a saying not found in the Old Testament, perhaps from a lost writing or Jesus speaking as God's Wisdom. Many scholars note that Ephesians' style and vocabulary differ from Paul's undisputed letters, but its ideas are clearly Pauline.

What it meant then. Honoring the tombs of prophets was a sign of piety, and Jesus turns it into an accusation. "Lawyers" or "scholars of the law" were scribes, the experts who interpreted the Law for the community. In Ephesians, the language of being "chosen" is borrowed from Israel's identity as God's chosen people, now opened to Gentiles too, which is the theme of the whole letter.

The allegorical readings. The fathers read the "key of knowledge" as the key of faith, which unlocks Scripture to show Christ. Teresa read the soul as a castle with seven sets of rooms; the journey of prayer is moving inward toward the King at the center.

What's debated or unknown. Scholars debate which Zechariah Jesus means: the priest stoned in 2 Chronicles 24, or the prophet Zechariah son of Berechiah (Matthew 23:35 adds that name). Authorship of Ephesians is still debated, as is whether the letter was written for Ephesus alone; the words "in Ephesus" are missing from some of the earliest manuscripts.
