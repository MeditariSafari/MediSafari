October 28th, 2026The five-minute version

The readings: On the plains of Moab, Moses seals the covenant. The people promise to walk in God's ways, and God promises that they will be his "treasured people." When they cross into the land, they're to write the whole law on huge plastered stones and celebrate with a feast. In Matthew, a young man comes to Jesus. He's kept all the commandments. "What do I still lack?" Jesus' answer cuts to the heart: sell what you have, give to the poor, and follow me. And the young man walks away sad, because he was very rich.

What it means: Israel is God's treasure, and the young man has a treasure of his own he can't put down. He's done everything right, but his heart is still held by what he owns. Jesus isn't adding a rule; he's inviting him to let go of what holds him, so he can be held by God instead. The question is the young man's own: what do I still lack?

Across the traditions: the same truth, in other words.

  • Seneca: "It is not the man who has too little, but the man who craves more, that is poor." (Letter 2, Gummere.)
  • Tao Te Ching: There is no calamity greater than not knowing when you have enough (Ch. 46, paraphrased).
  • Buddha: Craving is the root of suffering; letting go is the way to peace (the Four Noble Truths, paraphrased).
  • Sikh: Guru Nanak taught that wealth can't go with us when we die; only what we've given remains (paraphrased).
  • Islam: The Prophet said true wealth is not having many possessions, but richness of the soul (Bukhari, paraphrased).

Carry it today: What do you still lack, and what are you holding that keeps you from it?

The theme

  • Treasured people: God calls Israel his treasure.
  • Written in stone: The law is to be written where everyone entering the land will see it.
  • What do I still lack? The young man has done everything except the one thing.
  • Many possessions: He walks away grieving, held by what he owns.

The RCL has spent the week on Moses' last days and Israel's covenant. Today Jesus meets someone who keeps that covenant perfectly, and still lacks one thing.

1. This Very Day

"This very day the Lord your God is commanding you to observe these statutes and ordinances." "Today you have obtained the Lord's agreement: to be your God."

  • Today: Deuteronomy keeps saying "today." Every generation stands at the same moment of choice.
  • Mutual Promise: Both sides commit. The covenant is a relationship, not just a rulebook.

2. Treasured People

"Today the Lord has obtained your agreement: to be his treasured people."

  • Treasure: The Hebrew segullah means a king's personal treasure, kept close.
  • Chosen to Serve: Being treasured isn't privilege for its own sake; it's a calling to show God to the nations.

3. Written on Stones

"You shall write on them all the words of this law," and eat there, "rejoicing before the Lord your God."

  • Public Words: The law is posted at the entrance to the land for all to see.
  • Rejoicing: The covenant ends with a feast. Obedience and joy belong together.

4. What Do I Still Lack?

"Teacher, what good deed must I do to have eternal life?" "I have kept all these; what do I still lack?"

  • An Honest Question: The young man senses something missing, even after doing everything right.
  • Jesus Loves Him: Mark's version adds that Jesus looked at him and loved him (Mark 10:21, paraphrased).

5. He Went Away Grieving

"If you wish to be perfect, go, sell your possessions, and give the money to the poor, and you will have treasure in heaven; then come, follow me." "When the young man heard this word, he went away grieving, for he had many possessions." The young man came seeking one more good deed, and Jesus offered him freedom instead. The word "perfect" here means "complete, whole": the very thing he lacked. Israel was called God's treasure; the young man had his treasure in his possessions. Today's apostles, Simon and Jude, are among those who did leave everything to follow. The story doesn't tell us what the young man did later. His grief may have been the beginning of something. The question stays with every reader: what do I still lack?

For Christians today

The rich young man has been read in very different ways through Christian history.

  • The oldest readings: St. Anthony of Egypt heard this Gospel read in church around 270, sold everything and went into the desert, starting Christian monasticism. Clement of Alexandria wrote Who Is the Rich Man That Shall Be Saved?, arguing that Jesus asks for detachment of the heart, not necessarily giving away every coin.
  • Catholic: Catholic tradition distinguishes commandments, for all, from "counsels of perfection" like voluntary poverty, embraced by religious orders. St. Francis of Assisi built his life on this passage. Today's feast honors two apostles who left all to follow.
  • Protestant and evangelical: Most Protestants read the story as showing that no one can earn eternal life by keeping commandments; Jesus exposes what the young man trusts more than God. Many stress generosity and simple living as the fruit of faith.
  • Orthodox: The Orthodox see wealth as a gift to be shared, and the fathers (especially John Chrysostom and Basil) preached sharply that excess possessions belong to the poor.
  • Live debates today: How Christians should think about wealth, from prosperity preaching to voluntary poverty, is argued across traditions.

Carry it today: Give away one thing you're holding onto, quietly, to someone who needs it.

StoicismWiki ↗

What is Stoicism? A philosophy founded by Zeno of Citium around 300 BC in Athens, named for the painted porch (stoa) where he taught. Its most famous teachers are Epictetus, born into slavery; Seneca, a Roman statesman; and the emperor Marcus Aurelius. The Stoics taught that we can't control events, only our judgments and choices. Their four virtues are wisdom, courage, justice and self-control.

"It is not the man who has too little, but the man who craves more, that is poor." (Seneca, Letter 2, Gummere translation.) Seneca was one of the richest men in Rome, and he knew wealth's grip from the inside. He told his friend Lucilius that the measure of wealth is having what is necessary, and then having what is enough. The rich young man had more than enough and still lacked something; Jesus offered him the poverty that makes a person free.

The time. Deuteronomy's covenant ceremony is set on the plains of Moab; its final form dates to the 7th–6th centuries BC. Matthew wrote around AD 80–90.

What scholars broadly agree on. Deuteronomy 26–27 echoes ancient Near Eastern treaty ceremonies, with mutual oaths, written terms and a celebration. Mount Ebal, where the stones were to be set, is near Shechem; archaeologists have debated a structure found there since the 1980s. Matthew adapts Mark's story, calling the man "young" and adding Jesus' word "perfect."

What it meant then. For Israel, the covenant ceremony bound each generation anew to God. For Matthew's community, the rich young man was a warning that wealth can keep even the most observant from following Jesus.

The allegorical readings. The fathers read the plastered stones as hearts on which God writes the law (Jeremiah 31:33, 2 Corinthians 3:3).

What's debated or unknown. Whether Jesus' call to sell everything applies to all disciples or only some has been debated since the early church. The identification of the Mount Ebal structure as Joshua's altar is disputed.
