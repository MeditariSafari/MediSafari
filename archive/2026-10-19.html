October 19th, 2026The five-minute version

The readings: Two pictures. In Exodus, the people finally finish building the tabernacle, God's tent. A cloud covers it, and God's glory fills it so completely that even Moses can't go in. From then on, the cloud is their GPS: when it lifts, they pack up and move; when it stays, they stay, even if it's for days. In Revelation, an angel announces the fall of "Babylon," a code name for Rome, the richest city in the world. The kings and merchants who got rich from her weep as she burns in a single hour. A voice calls God's people: "Come out of her." And the psalmist, thirsty in the desert, says God's love is "better than life."

What it means: God chose to live in a tent that moved with the people, not in a palace. The people's job was simple and hard: follow the cloud. Don't run ahead, don't lag behind. Babylon is the opposite: a city that thought its wealth made it permanent, and it fell in an hour. The question for us: are we following God's lead, or building our own Babylon, a life organized around money, comfort and image, that can't last?

Across the traditions: the same truth, in other words.

  • Tao Te Ching: "It is better to leave a vessel unfilled, than to attempt to carry it when it is full."
  • Seneca: "It is not the man who has too little, but the man who craves more, that is poor."
  • Tao Te Ching: "Who can (make) the muddy water (clear)?" Only stillness and waiting (Ch. 15).
  • Buddha: Taught that all conditioned things are impermanent, and that clinging to them brings suffering (paraphrased).
  • Islam: the Qur'an warns that the rivalry for more distracts people "until you visit the graves" (102:1–2, paraphrased).

Carry it today: Where are you rushing ahead of God, and what are you holding on to that won't last?

The theme

  • Glory in a tent: God's glory fills a portable tent, not a palace. God travels with the people.
  • Follow the cloud: The Israelites moved only when the cloud lifted. Waiting was part of faithfulness.
  • Fallen, fallen: Babylon's wealth and power collapse "in one hour." What seemed permanent wasn't.
  • Come out of her: God calls the faithful to leave the empire's values, not just its streets.

All week the RCL has followed the tabernacle from plans to finished work. Today God moves in, and Revelation sets beside it a city that built for itself and fell. The Catholic Gospel's rich fool, who built bigger barns and died that night, makes the same point.

1. The Glory Filled the Tabernacle

"Then the cloud covered the tent of meeting, and the glory of the Lord filled the tabernacle."

  • The End of Exodus: The book began with Israel building store-cities for Pharaoh. It ends with them building a home for God, and God moving in.
  • Glory: The Hebrew word kavod means "weight" or "heaviness." God's glory is God's weighty, real presence.

2. Even Moses Could Not Enter

"Moses was not able to enter the tent of meeting because the cloud settled upon it."

  • Too Full: The man who spoke with God face to face has to wait outside. Holiness can't be managed, even by the best leader.
  • Echoes: The same thing happens when Solomon dedicates the Temple (1 Kings 8:10–11), and in Luke, a cloud overshadows Jesus and the disciples on the mountain.

3. When the Cloud Lifted

"Whenever the cloud was taken up from the tabernacle, the Israelites would set out on each stage of their journey; but if the cloud was not taken up, then they did not set out until the day that it was taken up."

  • Moving Together: God's presence wasn't a fixed shrine. It moved, and the people moved with it.
  • Waiting: Sometimes the cloud stayed for days, months or a year (Numbers 9:22). Waiting on God was part of the journey.

4. Fallen, Fallen Is Babylon

"Fallen, fallen is Babylon the great!" The merchants of the earth grew rich from her luxury, and kings and traders mourn as the city burns "in one hour."

  • Code Name: For John's readers, Babylon meant Rome, the empire that had destroyed Jerusalem as the old Babylon once had.
  • The Lament: The kings and merchants weep, not for the people, but for lost trade. Revelation lists the cargo: gold, silk, spices, horses, "slaves, and human souls" (18:13).

5. Come Out of Her, My People

"Come out of her, my people," so as not to share in her sins. The call isn't to flee a place but to refuse a way of life: luxury bought by exploitation, power that thinks it will never fall. Exodus ends with God living in a tent among a wandering people, following a cloud, waiting when it waits. Revelation ends with the great city of wealth in ashes and heaven rejoicing. The psalmist chooses between them: "your steadfast love is better than life." Jesus' rich fool in today's Catholic Gospel built bigger barns and died before morning. Every reading asks: will we live in God's moving tent, or build our own Babylon?

For Christians today

The cloud of glory has been read as a sign of God's presence in Christ and the church, and "Babylon" has been applied to nearly every power in history.

  • The oldest readings: The fathers saw the cloud filling the tabernacle as a figure of the incarnation: God's glory filling human flesh. John's Gospel says the Word "lived among us," literally "pitched his tent" (John 1:14), and "we have seen his glory."
  • Catholic: Catholic tradition sees the tabernacle in every church, where the Eucharist is kept, as the successor of the tent of meeting; the lamp burning beside it recalls the fire in the cloud. The Church's social teaching warns against the idolatry of money (Catechism §2424).
  • Protestant and evangelical: Reformers sometimes read "Babylon" as the papacy, a reading most Protestants have since set aside. Many evangelicals read Revelation 18 as a future world system, while mainline readers stress its critique of empire and economic injustice.
  • Orthodox: Orthodox Christians call Mary the "living tabernacle," filled with God's glory, and the "cloud" of God's presence is central to their theology of light. The Orthodox Church doesn't read Revelation in its liturgy.
  • Live debates today: Who is Babylon today? Christians debate how Revelation's critique applies to consumer economies, global trade and national power. And "following the cloud" raises the question of discernment: how do we know when God is moving, and when to wait?

Carry it today: Before one decision today, pause and ask whether you're following God's lead or running ahead.

StoicismWiki ↗

What is Stoicism? A philosophy founded by Zeno of Citium around 300 BC in Athens, named for the painted porch (stoa) where he taught. Its most famous teachers are Epictetus, born into slavery; Seneca, a Roman statesman; and the emperor Marcus Aurelius. The Stoics taught that we can't control events, only our judgments and choices. Their four virtues are wisdom, courage, justice and self-control.

"It is not the man who has too little, but the man who craves more, that is poor." (Seneca, Moral Letters to Lucilius 2, Gummere translation.) Seneca was one of the richest men in Rome, so he knew Babylon from the inside. He taught that wealth isn't measured by how much we have but by how little we need. Revelation's merchants weep over lost cargo; the rich fool builds bigger barns. Seneca's question for today: how much is enough?

Today's readings through Hindu and Jain eyes: Maha Navami and non-possession.

  • The Hindu lens: Today is Maha Navami, the ninth and last day of Navaratri, honoring Siddhidatri, "the giver of perfection." In much of India it is Ayudha Puja, when people honor the tools of their work, from books to cars, as gifts of the Goddess. Tomorrow is Vijayadashami (Dussehra), the victory of good over evil, when effigies of Ravana, king of the golden city of Lanka, are burned. Lanka, like Babylon, was a city of gold that fell.
  • The Jain lens: Aparigraha, non-possession, is the fifth of the Jain great vows: owning and wanting as little as possible, since attachment binds the soul.

Today is Shukla Navami, the ninth day of the bright half (Ashtami ends at 10:51 AM India time), with the moon moving from Uttara Ashadha into Shravana (per Indian calendars; exact Chicago times couldn't be confirmed).

What the words mean (all Sanskrit):

  • Navami: "the ninth" (from nava, "nine," compare Latin novem).
  • Siddhidatri: siddhi, "perfection, power," plus dātrī, "giver."
  • Ayudha: "weapon, tool."
  • Aparigraha: a-, "not," plus parigraha, "grasping on all sides."
  • Shravana: "hearing," linked to listening and learning.

The time. Exodus 40 belongs to the Priestly tradition, probably shaped during or after the Babylonian exile (6th century BC), when the people had lost their Temple and needed hope that God could live among them anywhere. Revelation was written around AD 95, under Emperor Domitian.

What scholars broadly agree on. Revelation's "Babylon" is Rome: the "seven mountains" (17:9) match Rome's seven hills, and Jewish writers after AD 70 called Rome "Babylon" because both empires destroyed Jerusalem's Temple. The list of cargo in Revelation 18 reads like a catalog of Rome's luxury imports. The lament borrows from Old Testament dirges over Babylon and Tyre (Isaiah 13, 23; Jeremiah 51; Ezekiel 27).

What it meant then. For exiles without a Temple, a portable tent filled with God's glory meant God wasn't tied to one place. For Christians under Rome, "Babylon has fallen" meant the empire's power wasn't the last word.

The allegorical readings. The fathers read the cloud as the Holy Spirit and its moving and resting as the Spirit's guidance of the church. "Babylon" became, for Augustine, the "earthly city," built on love of self, set against the "city of God," built on love of God.

What's debated or unknown. Scholars debate whether the tabernacle was a historical structure, a later idealized picture of the Temple, or both. They also debate whether Revelation was written under Domitian or earlier under Nero, and how much actual persecution Christians faced in the 90s.
