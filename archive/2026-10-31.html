October 31st, 2026The five-minute version

The readings: Rahab, who hid Joshua's spies, now lets them down by a rope from her window in Jericho's wall. They make her a promise: tie this crimson cord in your window, gather your family inside, and when Israel comes, you'll all be spared. She ties it right away. Meanwhile in Matthew, Jesus speaks his harshest words to religious leaders: woe to you, hypocrites! They tithe tiny garden herbs but neglect "justice and mercy and faith." They "strain out a gnat but swallow a camel." They're like whitewashed tombs: beautiful outside, full of death within. "First clean the inside of the cup."

What it means: Rahab's crimson cord is an outward sign of an inward faith; it means something because her heart is in it. The leaders Jesus condemns have the opposite problem: perfect outward signs with nothing inside. On All Hallows' Eve, when people put on masks, the readings ask what's behind ours.

Across the traditions: the same truth, in other words.

  • Marcus Aurelius: "Look within. Within is the fountain of good, and it will ever bubble up, if thou wilt ever dig." (Meditations 7.59, Long.)
  • Tao Te Ching: The sage clothes himself in coarse cloth but holds jade in his heart (Ch. 70, paraphrased).
  • Buddha: What good is matted hair or a deerskin robe, if inside you are a tangle of passions? You clean the outside only (Dhammapada 394, paraphrased).
  • Islam: The Prophet said God does not look at your bodies or your appearance, but at your hearts and deeds (Muslim, paraphrased).
  • Sikh: Guru Nanak said bathing at holy places means nothing if the heart is unclean (paraphrased).

Carry it today: What's behind your mask today, and does it match what people see?

The theme

  • The crimson cord: A small sign of a big faith: Rahab trusts the promise.
  • Woe to you: Jesus' sharpest words are for the religious, not the sinners.
  • Gnats and camels: Careful about small things, blind to the big ones.
  • Whitewashed tombs: Clean outside, death within.

All week the RCL followed Joshua's first steps. Tomorrow, All Saints' Day in many churches, honors the faithful who've gone before, people like Rahab.

1. Let Down by a Rope

"Then she let them down by a rope through the window," for her house was built into the city wall.

  • Escape: The spies escape the same way Paul later will, lowered in a basket through a wall (Acts 9:25).
  • Advice: Rahab tells them to hide in the hills for three days, protecting them even after they leave.

2. The Crimson Cord

The spies tell her to "tie this crimson cord in the window through which you let us down." "Then she tied the crimson cord in the window."

  • Like Passover: Like the blood on Israel's doorposts in Egypt, the scarlet sign marks a house to be spared.
  • At Once: Rahab ties it immediately, though Israel's attack is days away. Faith acts before it sees.

3. Woe to You

"But woe to you, scribes and Pharisees, hypocrites!" They have "neglected the weightier matters of the law: justice and mercy and faith."

  • The Weightier Matters: Jesus doesn't throw out the small commandments, but puts first things first.
  • Hypocrite: The Greek word means "stage actor," someone wearing a mask.

4. Gnats and Camels

"You blind guides! You strain out a gnat but swallow a camel!"

  • Humor: Jesus uses a ridiculous picture: carefully filtering out a tiny insect, then gulping down a camel.
  • A Pun: In Aramaic, "gnat" (qalma) and "camel" (gamla) sound alike.

5. Clean the Inside

"You blind Pharisee! First clean the inside of the cup and of the plate," for "you are like whitewashed tombs," beautiful outside, but inside full of death. In Jesus' day, tombs were whitewashed so travelers wouldn't touch them by accident and become ritually unclean, especially before festivals. Jesus turns the image around: the people most careful about outward purity can be the ones most full of death inside. Rahab, by contrast, was an outsider by every outward measure, but her heart was turned toward God, and her crimson cord showed it. Tonight, as children put on masks, and as Protestants remember Luther's call to reform a church that had grown rich in outward show, the question is the same: first clean the inside of the cup.

For Christians today

Matthew 23 has been both a call to self-examination and, sadly, a source of anti-Jewish prejudice.

  • The oldest readings: The fathers read Rahab's scarlet cord as a sign of Christ's blood, and her house as the Church, where all inside are saved. Origen applied Jesus' woes to Christian leaders, not to Jews.
  • Catholic: Today's Catholic Gospel, take the lowest place, makes the same point from another angle: humility inside, not honor outside. Catholic teaching since Vatican II (Nostra Aetate, 1965) rejects using passages like Matthew 23 against the Jewish people.
  • Protestant and evangelical: Today is Reformation Day. Luther's 95 Theses (1517) protested selling indulgences, an outward transaction in place of inward repentance. His first thesis said the whole life of believers should be repentance.
  • Orthodox: Today the Orthodox honor John Kochurov, who served at St. Vladimir's in Chicago and built Holy Trinity Cathedral there before his martyrdom in Russia.
  • Live debates today: Many scholars stress that the Pharisees were respected teachers, and that Matthew 23 reflects a bitter first-century family quarrel; churches now read it as a warning to themselves.

Carry it today: Do one good thing tonight that no one will see.

StoicismWiki ↗

What is Stoicism? A philosophy founded by Zeno of Citium around 300 BC in Athens, named for the painted porch (stoa) where he taught. Its most famous teachers are Epictetus, born into slavery; Seneca, a Roman statesman; and the emperor Marcus Aurelius. The Stoics taught that we can't control events, only our judgments and choices. Their four virtues are wisdom, courage, justice and self-control.

"Look within. Within is the fountain of good, and it will ever bubble up, if thou wilt ever dig." (Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 7.59, Long translation.) The emperor, surrounded by outward splendor, kept reminding himself that what matters is inside. Jesus tells the leaders to clean the inside of the cup first; Marcus says the inside is where the good springs up, if we keep digging.

The time. Joshua is set in the late 13th century BC; Matthew was written around AD 80–90, after the destruction of the Temple.

What scholars broadly agree on. Matthew 23 reflects tension between Matthew's Jewish-Christian community and the emerging rabbinic leadership after AD 70. The Pharisees themselves taught many of the same priorities Jesus names; the Talmud also criticizes hypocrites among the Pharisees. Whitewashing tombs before Passover is mentioned in later Jewish sources.

What it meant then. For Israel, Rahab's cord showed that a Canaanite could be spared by faith. For Matthew's readers, Jesus' woes warned against a religion of appearances, in any community.

The allegorical readings. The fathers saw the crimson cord as a sign of salvation through Christ's blood, like the Passover blood.

What's debated or unknown. Whether Jesus spoke all these woes at once or Matthew gathered them is debated. Archaeology doesn't support Jericho having walls in Joshua's time.
