“Moses' last charge: these words are not a trifle, they are your life. Jesus warns that you can study the words and still miss the life they point to.”
October 24th, 2026The five-minute version

The readings: Moses has just finished singing his farewell song. Now he speaks plainly: take these words to heart, teach them to your children, because "this is no trifling matter for you but rather your very life." Psalm 90, Moses' prayer, reminds us how short life is, like grass that grows in the morning and withers by evening, and asks God to make our work count. In John's Gospel, Jesus speaks to people who know the scriptures very well. "You search the scriptures," he says, "because you think that in them you have eternal life," but those very scriptures point to him, "yet you refuse to come to me to have life." Even Moses, he says, wrote about me.

What it means: It's possible to treat sacred words as a trifle in two ways: by ignoring them, or by studying them so hard that we never let them change us. Moses says the words are life itself. Jesus says the words point beyond themselves, to a living person. The point of reading is coming to the life the words describe.

Across the traditions: the same truth, in other words.

  • Epictetus: "I rather blush when I cannot show my actions agreeable and consonant to his discourse." (Enchiridion 49.)
  • Pirkei Avot: Rabbi Shimon ben Gamliel taught that study is not the main thing, but action (Avot 1:17, paraphrased).
  • Buddha: A person who recites much scripture but doesn't live it is like a cowherd counting other people's cows (Dhammapada 19, paraphrased).
  • Bhagavad Gita: To one who knows, all the Vedas are as useful as a well when the whole land is flooded (2:46, paraphrased).
  • Tao Te Ching: "The Tao that can be trodden is not the enduring and unchanging Tao." (Ch. 1, Legge.) The words point past themselves.

Carry it today: Which words have you studied for years but never let change you?

The theme

  • Take to heart: Moses wants the words inside them, not just in front of them.
  • Your very life: The words aren't extra; they're how the people will live in the land.
  • Searching: Jesus' hearers studied hard, and still missed him standing before them.
  • Glory: They sought "glory from one another" instead of from God. That's what blinded them.

The RCL has followed Moses all week, from the finished tabernacle to his song. Today he gives his last charge. Tomorrow, Sunday, he climbs Mount Nebo, sees the land and dies.

1. Take to Heart

"Take to heart all the words that I am giving in witness against you today; give them as a command to your children."

  • Heart, Not Head: In Hebrew, the heart is where we think and decide, not just feel. Moses wants the words to shape choices.
  • To Your Children: The words only last if they're handed on. Faith is always one generation from vanishing.

2. Your Very Life

"This is no trifling matter for you but rather your very life; through it you may live long in the land that you are crossing over the Jordan to possess."

  • Not Empty: The Hebrew word translated "trifling" means "empty." These words are full.
  • Life in the Land: For Moses, faithfulness and flourishing go together. Forgetting the words means losing the life.

3. Like Grass

"Like grass that is renewed in the morning; in the evening it fades and withers." "Satisfy us in the morning with your steadfast love, so that we may rejoice and be glad all our days."

  • Brief Lives: Psalm 90 is honest about how short life is. Moses himself won't cross the Jordan.
  • Morning Love: The answer to brevity isn't despair but asking God to fill each morning.

4. You Search the Scriptures

"You search the scriptures because you think that in them you have eternal life, and it is they that testify on my behalf. Yet you refuse to come to me to have life."

  • Careful Readers: Jesus doesn't fault their study. He faults stopping at the page.
  • Testimony: The scriptures are witnesses, pointing beyond themselves.

5. He Wrote About Me

"If you believed Moses, you would believe me, for he wrote about me. But if you do not believe what he wrote, how will you believe what I say?" Jesus turns the tables: the critics hope Moses is on their side, but Moses is their accuser, because Moses was pointing ahead all along. Christians have read the whole week's story this way: the tabernacle, the cloud, the cleft of the rock, the song, all pointing to a God who comes to live with people. The deeper problem Jesus names is glory: "How can you believe when you accept glory from one another and do not seek the glory that comes from the one who alone is God?" When we read to look wise, the words stay on the page. When we read to meet God, they become our very life.

For Christians today

Christians have always argued about how to read the Bible well, and this passage sits at the center of it.

  • The oldest readings: Early Christians read John 5:46 as a key for the whole Old Testament: Moses "wrote about me." Augustine said the New Testament is hidden in the Old, and the Old is revealed in the New.
  • Catholic: The Second Vatican Council's Dei Verbum (1965) says the Church venerates the scriptures as it venerates the Body of the Lord, and urges all the faithful to read them. St. Jerome is often quoted there: ignorance of scripture is ignorance of Christ.
  • Protestant and evangelical: The Reformers prized sola scriptura, scripture as the final authority, and Luther called the Bible "the cradle in which Christ is laid." Evangelicals stress daily Bible reading, and this verse is a reminder that the goal is relationship, not just knowledge.
  • Orthodox: Scripture is read within the Church's worship and tradition; the Gospel book itself is kissed and carried in procession. Today is a Memorial Saturday, when the Orthodox pray for the departed, reading Jesus' promise in John 5 that those who hear his voice will live.
  • Live debates today: How literally to read Moses' story, and how Christians should read the Hebrew Bible without erasing its meaning for Jews, are both live questions.

Carry it today: Read one short passage slowly, and ask not what it means, but who it points you to.

StoicismWiki ↗

What is Stoicism? A philosophy founded by Zeno of Citium around 300 BC in Athens, named for the painted porch (stoa) where he taught. Its most famous teachers are Epictetus, born into slavery; Seneca, a Roman statesman; and the emperor Marcus Aurelius. The Stoics taught that we can't control events, only our judgments and choices. Their four virtues are wisdom, courage, justice and self-control.

"But if I admire nothing but merely the interpretation, what do I become more than a grammarian instead of a philosopher? ... When anyone, therefore, desires me to read Chrysippus to him, I rather blush when I cannot show my actions agreeable and consonant to his discourse." (Epictetus, Enchiridion 49.) Epictetus is talking about his school's great books, the works of Chrysippus. Being able to explain them is nothing to boast about, he says; the only thing that matters is living by them. It's the same warning Jesus gives in John 5: you can search the scriptures diligently and still not come to the life they point to.

The time. Deuteronomy sets these words on the plains of Moab, at the end of Moses' life, traditionally in the 13th century BC. Most scholars think Deuteronomy took shape in the 7th century BC, around King Josiah's reforms, and was completed during or after the exile. John's Gospel was likely written around AD 90–100.

What scholars broadly agree on. Deuteronomy is framed as Moses' farewell speeches, and its repeated call to "teach your children" reflects a community working to pass on its faith. John 5 belongs to a series of debates set around Jewish festivals; its sharp language about "the Jews" reflects tensions between John's community and the synagogue near the end of the first century.

What it meant then. For Israel, Moses' words were a charter for life in the land and an explanation for why they lost it. For John's readers, likely Jewish believers in Jesus pushed out of their synagogues, Jesus' words reassured them that Moses and the scriptures were on their side.

The allegorical readings. Origen and others read Moses dying outside the land as a sign that the law alone could not bring the people home; Joshua, whose name is the Hebrew form of "Jesus," leads them in.

What's debated or unknown. Scholars debate how much of Deuteronomy reflects older tradition. How to read John's polemical language responsibly, given its later use against Jews, is an important discussion in churches today.
