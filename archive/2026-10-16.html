October 16th, 2026The five-minute version

The readings: Moses has been given the plans for the tabernacle, the tent where God will live among the people. Now God names who will build it. "See, I have called by name Bezalel," and "I have filled him with a divine spirit, with ability, intelligence, and knowledge, and every kind of skill": skill to work gold, silver, bronze, stone and wood. God gives him a partner, Oholiab, and gives "skill to all the skillful." In 1 Peter, an elder writes to church leaders: take care of God's people willingly, not for money; don't boss them around; "be examples to the flock." And to everyone: "God opposes the proud but gives grace to the humble."

What it means: This is the first time in the Bible that someone is "filled with a divine spirit," and it's not a prophet or a king. It's a craftsman. God's spirit shows up in skilled hands. Every gift, whether it's carpentry, teaching, cooking or caring for people, is meant to build a place where God can live among us. Peter adds how to use any gift that gives us influence: as a shepherd and an example, never as a boss. Gifts are for building, and leaders are for serving.

Across the traditions: the same truth, in other words.

  • Kahlil Gibran: "When you work you are a flute through whose heart the whispering of the hours turns to music."
  • Tao Te Ching: the sage "wishing to be above men, puts himself by his words below them."
  • Confucius: governing by virtue is like "the north polar star."
  • Bhagavad Gita: better to do your own work, even imperfectly, than another's well.
  • Bahá'í: work done in the spirit of service is worship.

Carry it today: What gift were you given, and what are you building with it?

The theme

  • Called by name: God doesn't call Bezalel a generic worker; he knows his name, his father and his tribe. Every calling is personal.
  • Spirit in skilled hands: The first person "filled with a divine spirit" in the Bible is an artist. Craft can be holy.
  • Gifts are for building: The skill is given "so that they may make all that I have commanded." Gifts point beyond the person who has them.
  • Examples, not lords: Peter's rule for anyone with influence: serve willingly, lead humbly, show the way.

This week's RCL readings have followed Israel after the golden calf: broken tablets, mourning, and Moses meeting God "face to face" in the tent. Today the story steps back to the plans for God's dwelling, and to the people gifted to build it.

1. Called by Name

"See, I have called by name Bezalel son of Uri son of Hur, of the tribe of Judah."

  • Known Personally: God names Bezalel, his father, his grandfather and his tribe. This isn't a job posting; it's a calling.
  • A Name That Fits: Bezalel means "in the shadow (or protection) of God," a fitting name for someone who will build God's dwelling.

2. Filled with a Divine Spirit

"I have filled him with a divine spirit, with ability, intelligence, and knowledge, and every kind of skill."

  • The First Time: This is the first person in the Bible said to be filled with God's spirit. Not a prophet, priest or king: a craftsman.
  • Head and Hands: The gifts include ability, intelligence and knowledge, practical wisdom and know-how together. Making beautiful things is a spiritual work.

3. Skill for All the Skillful

"And I have given skill to all the skillful, so that they may make all that I have commanded you."

  • A Team: Bezalel isn't alone. God gives him Oholiab and a whole crew. No one builds God's house single-handed.
  • Gifts with a Purpose: The skill is given for something: the tent, the ark, the lampstand, the priests' garments. Gifts aren't trophies; they're tools.

4. Tend the Flock

"Tend the flock of God that is in your charge... Do not lord it over those in your charge, but be examples to the flock."

  • Willingly: Peter tells elders to lead "not under compulsion but willingly," not for money but eagerly.
  • Example Over Control: The image is a shepherd walking ahead of the sheep, not a boss driving them from behind. Jesus himself is "the chief shepherd" (5:4).

5. Clothed with Humility

"And all of you must clothe yourselves with humility in your dealings with one another, for 'God opposes the proud but gives grace to the humble.'" Peter quotes Proverbs 3:34, the same verse James quotes (James 4:6). The Greek word for "clothe" is used of tying on a servant's apron, perhaps a memory of Jesus tying on a towel to wash his disciples' feet. The craftsmen in Exodus and the elders in Peter are doing the same thing: using what God gave them to build up a community where God can live. Bezalel's gold and Peter's shepherding both come down to this: the gift is God's, the work is service, and the right clothing is humility.

For Christians today

Exodus 31 has become a favorite text for Christian artists, and 1 Peter 5 for anyone thinking about church leadership.

  • The oldest readings: Early Christian writers read the tabernacle as a picture of Christ and of the Church, and its craftsmen as an image of the Holy Spirit giving gifts to build up the body of Christ (1 Corinthians 12). John's Gospel says the Word "lived among us," literally "pitched his tent" or "tabernacled" (John 1:14).
  • Catholic: Catholic teaching speaks of the "charisms," gifts of the Spirit given to every believer for the good of the Church (Catechism §799–801). Pope John Paul II's 1999 Letter to Artists said artists share in God's creative work.
  • Protestant and evangelical: Luther and Calvin taught the "priesthood of all believers" and the dignity of every honest vocation: the cobbler and the farmer serve God as truly as the priest. Many Protestant churches use "spiritual gifts" inventories to help members find their calling. Methodist and Presbyterian churches often read 1 Peter 5 at the ordination of elders.
  • Orthodox: Icon painters in the Orthodox tradition traditionally pray and fast as they work, seeing their craft as a ministry; Bezalel is sometimes called the first iconographer.
  • Live debates today: "Do not lord it over" is at the center of today's conversations about spiritual abuse and authoritarian church leadership. Many denominations are re-examining how pastors use power, often with 1 Peter 5:3 as the standard.

StoicismWiki ↗

What is Stoicism? A philosophy founded by Zeno of Citium around 300 BC in Athens, named for the painted porch (stoa) where he taught. Its most famous teachers are Epictetus, born into slavery; Seneca, a Roman statesman; and the emperor Marcus Aurelius. The Stoics taught that we can't control events, only our judgments and choices. Their four virtues are wisdom, courage, justice and self-control.

"Take care that thou art not made into a Caesar, that thou art not dyed with this dye; for such things happen." (Marcus Aurelius, Meditations Book 6, Long translation.) The most powerful man in the world reminds himself not to let power stain him. He goes on: "Keep thyself then simple, good, pure, serious, free from affectation, a friend of justice, a worshipper of the gods, kind, affectionate, strenuous in all proper acts." It's Peter's "do not lord it over those in your charge," from the emperor's own pen. And it's a fitting lead-in to Sunday's Gospel, where Jesus is asked about paying taxes to Caesar.

The time. Exodus is set around the 13th century BC, but scholars think the book took its final shape much later, during or after the Babylonian exile (6th–5th century BC). 1 Peter was written between about AD 60 and 90, to Christian communities in five Roman provinces of Asia Minor.

What scholars broadly agree on. The tabernacle chapters (Exodus 25–31 and 35–40) belong to what scholars call the Priestly source, which cared deeply about worship, holiness and God's presence. Many think the portable tent was described partly in light of the later Temple in Jerusalem. Most scholars think 1 Peter was written by a follower of Peter in his name, possibly from Rome (which the letter calls "Babylon," 5:13), though some hold that Peter wrote it with help from Silvanus (5:12).

What it meant then. For exiles who had lost the Temple, the tabernacle stories told them that God could dwell among his people anywhere, even in the wilderness. Skilled craftsmen in the ancient Near East were valued but rarely honored; Exodus gives one by name the same spirit later given to prophets and kings. For 1 Peter's readers, small persecuted house churches, good leadership could hold a community together or break it.

The allegorical readings. The fathers read the tabernacle's materials symbolically: gold for divinity, wood for humanity, together in Christ. Bezalel was read as a figure of Christ the builder of the Church, and the skilled workers as the Spirit's gifts in believers.

What's debated or unknown. Scholars debate whether a tent as elaborate as Exodus describes could have been built in the wilderness, or whether the description is idealized. The authorship and date of 1 Peter remain disputed. The exact roles of "elders" in early churches, and how they related to later bishops and priests, are still debated across denominations.
