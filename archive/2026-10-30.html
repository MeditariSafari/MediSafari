October 30th, 2026The five-minute version

The readings: Joshua sends two spies into Jericho, the first city across the Jordan. They end up at the house of Rahab, a prostitute whose home is built into the city wall. When the king sends soldiers, Rahab hides the spies under stalks of flax on her roof and sends the soldiers off on a false trail. Then she makes a remarkable confession: "I know that the Lord has given you the land." "The Lord your God is indeed God in heaven above and on earth below." She asks only that they spare her family. The first person in the promised land to confess Israel's God is a Canaanite woman on the margins of her city.

What it means: Rahab had every reason to be left out of the story: a foreigner, a prostitute, an enemy. Yet her courage and faith save the spies, and she becomes part of Israel. Matthew names her in Jesus' family tree. God's people are often sheltered by the people we'd least expect.

Across the traditions: the same truth, in other words.

  • Seneca: "'They are slaves,' people declare. Nay, rather they are men." (Letter 47, Gummere.)
  • Islam: The Prophet told of a sinful woman forgiven by God because she gave water to a thirsty dog (Bukhari, paraphrased).
  • Buddha: Ambapali, a wealthy courtesan, hosted the Buddha and gave her mango grove to the community; she later became a nun (Mahāparinibbāna Sutta, paraphrased).
  • Sikh: The Gurus rejected caste: Guru Nanak said he stood with the lowest of the low (paraphrased).
  • Hindu: The Ramayana tells of Shabari, a tribal woman, whose humble offering of berries Rama gladly ate (paraphrased).

Carry it today: Who have you written off who might be the very person God is working through?

The theme

  • The outsider: Rahab is foreign, female and a prostitute: three strikes in the ancient world.
  • Courage: She risks her life to hide the spies.
  • Confession: She names Israel's God before any Israelite in the land does.
  • Family: She asks for mercy for her whole household.

Yesterday Joshua took up Moses' work; today his spies find faith in an unexpected place. Sunday, the people cross the Jordan on dry ground.

1. Two Spies

"Then Joshua son of Nun sent two men secretly from Shittim as spies," and they "entered the house of a prostitute whose name was Rahab."

  • A House in the Wall: Rahab's home was part of Jericho's wall, on the edge of the city in every sense.
  • Why There? An inn or brothel was a place strangers could come and go without notice.

2. Hidden in the Flax

"But the woman took the two men and hid them." "She had, however, brought them up to the roof and hidden them with the stalks of flax."

  • Risk: Hiding enemy spies could cost her life.
  • Ordinary Things: Flax, drying on the roof for linen, becomes the means of rescue.

3. I Know

"I know that the Lord has given you the land." "The Lord your God is indeed God in heaven above and on earth below."

  • Faith from Hearing: Rahab has only heard the stories of the Red Sea and the wilderness, and she believes.
  • A Full Confession: Her words echo Moses' own in Deuteronomy 4:39.

4. Deal Kindly

She asks them to "deal kindly with my family," and they promise to do so "when the Lord gives us the land."

  • Hesed: The Hebrew word for "kindly" is hesed, the faithful love God shows Israel. Rahab asks for the same love.
  • The Scarlet Cord: She will tie a red cord in her window as a sign, and her household will be spared (Joshua 2:18, paraphrased).

5. Unexpected Faith

The New Testament remembers Rahab three times: in Jesus' genealogy as the mother of Boaz (Matthew 1:5), among the heroes of faith (Hebrews 11:31), and as an example of faith shown in action (James 2:25). The outsider becomes an ancestor of David, and of Jesus. Meanwhile 2 Peter warns about the opposite: insiders, teachers among God's people, who exploit others for gain. Faith isn't a matter of belonging to the right group. Today's Catholic Gospel shows Jesus healing at a Pharisee's table while the respectable guests look on silently. Rahab reminds us to watch for God in the people at the edges.

For Christians today

Rahab is one of the most surprising figures in the Bible, and Christians have honored her for centuries.

  • The oldest readings: Clement of Rome (around AD 96) praised Rahab for faith and hospitality, and read her scarlet cord as a sign of redemption through Christ's blood. Many fathers saw her as a figure of the Church gathered from the nations.
  • Catholic: Rahab is honored as one of the four women in Matthew's genealogy, along with Tamar, Ruth and Bathsheba, all outsiders or women with complicated stories.
  • Protestant and evangelical: Rahab is often preached as proof that no one is beyond God's grace, and her "lie" to the soldiers has prompted long debates about honesty and protecting life.
  • Orthodox: The Orthodox honor Rahab as a righteous woman, and Maryam of Qidun, commemorated today in the Episcopal calendar, is a beloved Eastern saint of repentance.
  • Live debates today: Rahab's deception raises ethical questions: is it right to lie to protect the innocent? Christians in Nazi Europe who hid Jews often cited her example.

Carry it today: Welcome someone you'd normally keep at the edge of your circle.

StoicismWiki ↗

What is Stoicism? A philosophy founded by Zeno of Citium around 300 BC in Athens, named for the painted porch (stoa) where he taught. Its most famous teachers are Epictetus, born into slavery; Seneca, a Roman statesman; and the emperor Marcus Aurelius. The Stoics taught that we can't control events, only our judgments and choices. Their four virtues are wisdom, courage, justice and self-control.

"'They are slaves,' people declare. Nay, rather they are men... Kindly remember that he whom you call your slave sprang from the same stock." (Seneca, Letter 47, Gummere translation.) In a society built on slavery, Seneca told his friend Lucilius to look at the people his world despised and see human beings, born of the same stock, under the same sky. It's the same lesson as Rahab: the label others give someone doesn't tell you who they are, or what God may do through them.

The time. Joshua is set in the late 13th century BC; the book was likely written centuries later. 2 Peter is usually dated to the early 2nd century, written in Peter's name.

What scholars broadly agree on. Excavations at Jericho (Tell es-Sultan) show the city was largely unoccupied in the late Bronze Age, which makes the conquest story difficult to match with archaeology. Rahab's story may preserve a memory of a Canaanite clan that joined Israel; the text says her family lived among Israel "to this day" (Joshua 6:25).

What it meant then. For Israel, Rahab showed that foreigners who confessed Israel's God could join the people. For 2 Peter's readers, false teachers were a danger from inside the community.

The allegorical readings. The fathers read the scarlet cord in Rahab's window as Christ's blood, and her house as the Church: all who are inside are saved.

What's debated or unknown. Some ancient interpreters (and Josephus) called Rahab an innkeeper rather than a prostitute. Whether the conquest of Jericho happened as told is strongly debated.
