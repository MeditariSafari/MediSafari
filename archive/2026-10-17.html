“Faithful work, finished and blessed, or power used to save face. Today shows both kinds of builder.”
October 17th, 2026The five-minute version

The readings: Two scenes, two kinds of leader. In Exodus, months of work end: the people bring every finished piece of the tabernacle to Moses, the tent, the ark, the lampstand and the priests' clothes, and it's all done just as God commanded. Moses looks it over and blesses them. In Matthew, King Herod throws a birthday party. Herod has jailed John the Baptist for saying his marriage is wrong. His stepdaughter dances, he promises her anything, and her mother tells her to ask for John's head. Herod is upset, but in front of his guests he won't back down. John is killed in prison.

What it means: Moses' people worked together, finished what they were given, and were blessed. Herod had all the power in the room, and he used it to protect his image. He knew killing John was wrong; he "was grieved." But he cared more about his oath and his guests than about doing right. John, with no power at all, told the truth and paid for it. The question for us: are we finishing the good work we were given, or are we, like Herod, sometimes more worried about saving face than doing right?

Across the traditions: the same truth, in other words.

  • Confucius: "To see what is right and not to do it is want of courage."
  • Buddha: "As a solid rock is not shaken by the wind, wise people falter not amidst blame and praise."
  • Tao Te Ching: "When the work is done, and one's name is becoming distinguished, to withdraw into obscurity is the way of Heaven."
  • Epictetus: Helvidius Priscus told the emperor, "it is your part to kill; it is mine to die, but not in fear."
  • Kahlil Gibran: "And all work is empty save when there is love."

Carry it today: Is there something right you've been avoiding because of what others might think?

The theme

  • Finished and blessed: The people did "all the work as the Lord had commanded," and Moses blessed them. Faithful work ends in blessing.
  • The truth-teller: John told the most powerful man in Galilee the truth about his marriage, knowing what it might cost.
  • Trapped by pride: Herod "was grieved," but "out of regard for his oaths and for the guests," he did it anyway. Saving face can cost someone their life.
  • Two builders: Moses' people built a home for God. Herod built a banquet for his own image. Both stories ask what we're building with our power.

This week the RCL has followed the tabernacle from plans to finished work. Today it's done, and Matthew sets beside it a king who built nothing but his own reputation, preparing for tomorrow's question about what belongs to Caesar and what belongs to God.

1. All the Work Was Finished

"Thus all the work of the tabernacle of the tent of meeting was finished; the Israelites had done everything just as the Lord had commanded Moses."

  • A Whole People: The work wasn't done by Moses alone, or even Bezalel alone. Everyone who was "willing of heart" brought gifts and labor (Exodus 35).
  • Finished: The Hebrew word for "finished" (kalah) is the same one used when God finished creation (Genesis 2:1–2). The tabernacle is a small world made for God to live in.

2. Moses Saw, and Blessed Them

"When Moses saw that they had done all the work as the Lord had commanded, he blessed them."

  • Echoes of Creation: God saw all he had made and called it very good, then blessed the seventh day. Moses sees, and blesses.
  • Leaders Who Bless: Moses doesn't take credit; he blesses the workers. Good leadership notices faithful work and honors it.

3. John and Herod

"Herod had arrested John, bound him, and put him in prison on account of Herodias, his brother Philip's wife, because John had been telling him, 'It is not lawful for you to have her.'"

  • Truth to Power: John didn't flatter the king. He named what was wrong, publicly and repeatedly.
  • A Fearful King: Herod was afraid of the crowds, who considered John a prophet, so he locked John away instead of killing him outright.

4. Out of Regard for His Oaths and Guests

"The king was grieved, yet out of regard for his oaths and for the guests, he commanded it to be given; he sent and had John beheaded in the prison."

  • The Trap of a Rash Promise: Herod promised "whatever she might ask," and his pride wouldn't let him take it back.
  • Image over Conscience: He was sorry, but not sorry enough to look weak in front of his guests. Power used to save face is the opposite of the tabernacle's faithful work.

5. They Went and Told Jesus

"His disciples came and took the body and buried him; then they went and told Jesus." John's story ends in grief and faithfulness: his followers give him an honorable burial and bring their sorrow to Jesus. Matthew says that when Jesus heard, "he withdrew from there in a boat to a deserted place by himself" (14:13), and then, when the crowds followed, he fed five thousand people. Herod's banquet ends in death; Jesus' meal in the wilderness ends in abundance. The tabernacle finished in faithfulness and Herod's feast ending in murder are a sharp contrast: what we build with our power either makes room for God and others, or makes room only for ourselves. Tomorrow's Gospel asks the same thing with a coin: what belongs to Caesar, and what belongs to God?

For Christians today

John the Baptist is honored as a martyr for truth, and the finished tabernacle has long been read as a picture of God dwelling with people.

  • The oldest readings: Early Christians saw John as the last and greatest of the prophets, who died for speaking God's truth to a king. Ignatius of Antioch, honored today, wrote on his way to martyrdom that he wanted to be found faithful to the end, "God's wheat," an echo of John's courage.
  • Catholic: The Church celebrates the Passion of St. John the Baptist on August 29. The Catechism calls him "more than a prophet" and the one who "bears witness to Christ" by his preaching, baptism and martyrdom (§523, §719).
  • Protestant and evangelical: Protestant preachers have long held up John as a model of prophetic courage, from Reformation-era preachers who confronted rulers to Dietrich Bonhoeffer, who was executed in 1945 for resisting Hitler. Calvin wrote that Herod's oath was no excuse: a sinful promise must be broken, not kept.
  • Orthodox: The Orthodox Church honors the Beheading of John the Baptist (August 29) as a strict fast day. Icons show the finished tabernacle as a type of the Virgin Mary, who carried God within her.
  • Live debates today: "Speaking truth to power" is one of the most debated callings in churches today: when should Christians publicly confront political leaders, and when does that become partisanship? Herod's choice also raises the question of keeping promises: Christian ethics generally holds that a vow to do wrong should be broken.

Carry it today: Finish one small piece of good work you've left undone, and bless someone else's finished work with a word of thanks.

Today's readings through Hindu and Jain eyes: Maha Saptami and fearless truth.

  • The Hindu lens: Today is Maha Saptami, the seventh day of Navaratri, honoring Kalaratri, the fiercest form of the Goddess, dark as night, who destroys fear itself. In Bengal and across eastern India, Durga Puja's main days begin today. The Hindu ideal of satya, truthfulness, is one of the highest virtues; the Upanishads say "truth alone triumphs" (satyameva jayate, Mundaka Upanishad 3.1.6), now India's national motto.
  • The Jain lens: Jains take a vow of satya, truthfulness, as the second of their five great vows, but always joined with ahimsa: truth spoken without cruelty. Saturday belongs to Shani (Saturn), who in Hindu tradition rewards faithful work and punishes misused power.

Today is Shukla Saptami, the seventh day of the bright half (Shashthi ends at 5:54 AM India time), with the moon moving from Mula into Purva Ashadha (per Indian calendars; exact Chicago times couldn't be confirmed).

What the words mean (all Sanskrit):

  • Kalaratri: kāla, "time, death, black," plus rātri, "night": "the night of time."
  • Satya: "truth," from sat, "being, what is real."
  • Satyameva jayate: "truth alone conquers."
  • Saptami: "the seventh" (from sapta, "seven," compare Latin septem).
  • Purva Ashadha: "the former invincible one."

StoicismWiki ↗

What is Stoicism? A philosophy founded by Zeno of Citium around 300 BC in Athens, named for the painted porch (stoa) where he taught. Its most famous teachers are Epictetus, born into slavery; Seneca, a Roman statesman; and the emperor Marcus Aurelius. The Stoics taught that we can't control events, only our judgments and choices. Their four virtues are wisdom, courage, justice and self-control.

When the emperor Vespasian ordered the senator Helvidius Priscus not to come to the senate, Priscus replied, "It is in your power not to allow me to be a member of the senate, but so long as I am, I must go in." Threatened with death, he answered, "it is your part to kill; it is mine to die, but not in fear." (Epictetus, Discourses 1.2, Long translation.) Epictetus tells this story as a model of keeping one's integrity before power. Priscus was later executed. He, like John the Baptist, understood that a ruler can take your life but not your character. Herod is the opposite: he had the power, but he couldn't stand up to his own guests.

The time. Exodus is set around the 13th century BC; the tabernacle chapters belong to the Priestly tradition, probably shaped during or after the Babylonian exile. Herod Antipas ruled Galilee and Perea from 4 BC to AD 39. John the Baptist was killed around AD 28–29.

What scholars broadly agree on. The Jewish historian Josephus, writing around AD 93, confirms that Herod Antipas imprisoned and executed John at the fortress of Machaerus, east of the Dead Sea. Josephus says Herod feared John's popularity might lead to a revolt, a political reason the Gospels mention but don't stress. Josephus also says many Jews saw Herod's later military defeat as God's punishment for killing John.

What it meant then. Herodias had been married to Herod's half-brother (Josephus calls him Herod; Matthew and Mark call him Philip). Leviticus 18:16 and 20:21 forbade marrying a brother's wife while the brother was alive, so John's charge was grounded in the Law. For Matthew's readers, John's death foreshadows Jesus' own: both are innocent, both are killed by rulers who hesitate but give in.

The allegorical readings. The fathers read the finished tabernacle as a figure of the Church, built from the gifts of many, and of Christ's body. Some read Herod's banquet as the world's feast, ending in death, set against Christ's feast of the loaves, ending in life.

What's debated or unknown. Josephus doesn't mention the dance or the banquet, so some scholars think those details are popular storytelling; others think they preserve real memories. The dancer's name, Salome, comes from Josephus, not the Gospels, and her age is unknown. Scholars also debate how an elaborate tabernacle could have been built in the wilderness, or whether the account describes an ideal.
