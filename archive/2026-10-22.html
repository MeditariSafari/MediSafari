“Moses' time is ending. He hands on his leadership, and a song, to a people God knows will stray. What will we leave behind?.”
October 22nd, 2026The five-minute version

The readings: Moses' life is ending. God calls him and Joshua to the tent of meeting, where the cloud of God's presence appears one more time. God gives Moses hard news: after you're gone, the people will drift to other gods, and I will hide my face from them when trouble comes. So God gives Moses one last job: write a song and teach it to them, so that when things go wrong, the song will be there to remind them who they are and who God is. Moses writes it that same day. Psalm 90, the "prayer of Moses," looks at life from the end: our years pass like grass, but God has been our home in every generation. And the letter to Titus warns about people who say they know God but whose lives say otherwise.

What it means: Moses doesn't get to finish the journey. He hands it on: leadership to Joshua, memory to the people in a song. He knows they'll fail, and God knows it too, but they give the people something to come back to. That's what a good ending looks like: not a perfect finish, but a faithful handoff. And Titus asks whether our lives sing the same song as our words.

Across the traditions: the same truth, in other words.

  • Epictetus: "Let death and exile, and all other things which appear terrible be daily before your eyes, but chiefly death, and you [will] never entertain any abject thought."
  • Psalm 90 (the "Prayer of Moses"): "For a thousand years in your sight are like yesterday when it is past or like a watch in the night."
  • Buddha: Taught that remembering death (maraṇasati) frees the mind from clinging (paraphrased).
  • Confucius: Said that if you don't yet understand life, how can you understand death? (Analects 11.12, paraphrased.)
  • Islam: the Qur'an says, "Every soul will taste death" (3:185, paraphrased).

Carry it today: If you knew your time was short, what "song" would you want to leave the people you love?

The theme

  • Your time is near: Moses has led for forty years, and he won't see the promised land. He hands it on.
  • Call Joshua: The work continues through someone else. A good leader prepares a successor.
  • Write this song: God knows the people will forget, so he gives them a song to remember by.
  • Deny him by their actions: Titus warns that faith must be lived, not just professed.

This week the RCL has followed Israel from the finished tabernacle to the border of the promised land. Now, at the end of Moses' story, he passes the journey on. Sunday's readings will tell of his death on Mount Nebo.

1. Your Time to Die Is Near

"Your time to die is near; call Joshua and present yourselves in the tent of meeting."

  • No Denial: God speaks plainly to Moses about his death, and Moses accepts it.
  • Not Alone: Moses doesn't go to the tent alone; he brings his successor. Endings are shared.

2. The Pillar of Cloud

"The Lord appeared at the tent in a pillar of cloud; the pillar of cloud stood at the entrance to the tent."

  • The Same Presence: The cloud that led them out of Egypt and filled the tabernacle is there at the end.
  • Continuity: The cloud stands at the entrance, between the old leader and the new.

3. I Will Hide My Face

"Then this people will begin to prostitute themselves to the foreign gods in their midst," and "I will forsake them and hide my face from them."

  • Hard Truth: God doesn't sugarcoat the future. The people will fail.
  • Hiddenness: "Hiding the face" (hester panim) became a key Jewish phrase for times when God seems absent, especially after great tragedies.

4. Write This Song

"Now, therefore, write this song, and teach it to the Israelites." "For I know what they are inclined to do even now." "That very day Moses wrote this song and taught it to the Israelites."

  • A Song as Witness: God gives the people a song they'll still be singing when they've forgotten everything else. Songs outlast sermons.
  • Grace Before Failure: God gives the remedy before the sickness. Knowing they'll stray, God gives them a way back.

5. Prosper the Work of Our Hands

"Lord, you have been our dwelling place in all generations." "For a thousand years in your sight are like yesterday when it is past." "Let the favor of the Lord our God be upon us and prosper for us the work of our hands—O prosper the work of our hands!" Psalm 90 is the prayer of a man who knows his time is short, and it doesn't despair. It asks for one thing: that what we did with our brief lives would last, because God is our home across generations. Titus warns against the opposite, people who "profess to know God, but they deny him by their actions." Moses' legacy isn't a monument; it's a song, a successor and a faithful life. Ours can be the same: something sung, someone prepared, and a life that matches our words.

For Christians today

Moses' death and his song have shaped Christian thinking about legacy, leadership and dying well.

  • The oldest readings: The fathers read Joshua (Iēsous in Greek) as a figure of Christ, who leads God's people into the land Moses could only see. The Song of Moses (Deuteronomy 32) was sung in the early church, and Revelation 15:3 has the saints singing "the song of Moses... and the song of the Lamb."
  • Catholic: Catholic tradition encourages preparing for a good death (ars moriendi), and has a patron for it, St. Joseph. St. John Paul II, honored today, publicly embraced his own aging and illness as a witness.
  • Protestant and evangelical: Psalm 90 is the classic Protestant funeral psalm, paraphrased in Isaac Watts's hymn "O God, Our Help in Ages Past" (1719). Evangelicals often stress Titus's call to integrity in leaders.
  • Orthodox: The Orthodox Church honors Moses as a saint (September 4) and sings the Song of Moses as the second biblical ode in its morning prayers. Orthodox spirituality calls for "remembrance of death" as a daily practice.
  • Live debates today: How should churches handle leadership succession, especially when founders stay too long? And Titus's lists raise debates about who can be a church leader, including questions of gender and marriage that divide churches today.

Carry it today: Write down one thing you'd want someone you love to remember about God, and tell them.

What is Stoicism? A philosophy founded by Zeno of Citium around 300 BC in Athens, named for the painted porch (stoa) where he taught. Its most famous teachers are Epictetus, born into slavery; Seneca, a Roman statesman; and the emperor Marcus Aurelius. The Stoics taught that we can't control events, only our judgments and choices. Their four virtues are wisdom, courage, justice and self-control.

"Let death and exile, and all other things which appear terrible be daily before your eyes, but chiefly death, and you [will] never entertain any abject thought, nor too eagerly covet anything." (Epictetus, Enchiridion 21, Carter translation.) The Stoics practiced memento mori, remembering death, not to be gloomy but to live fully and freely. Moses, told his time was near, didn't panic; he wrote a song and taught it that same day. Remembering death clarifies what matters.

Today's readings through Hindu and Jain eyes: Ekadashi and passing on.

  • The Hindu lens: Today is Papankusha Ekadashi, a fast day dedicated to Vishnu, said to restrain sin like a goad restrains an elephant. In the Mahabharata, the dying elder Bhishma, lying on a bed of arrows, teaches Yudhishthira for days before he dies, a deathbed handoff of wisdom like Moses' song.
  • The Jain lens: Jains honor sallekhana, a carefully prepared, peaceful death through gradual fasting, undertaken with permission and supervision when death is near. Its goal is to meet death calmly and consciously.

Today is Shukla Ekadashi (ending at 2:48 PM India time), then Dvadashi, with the moon moving from Shatabhisha into Purva Bhadrapada (per Indian calendars; exact Chicago times couldn't be confirmed).

What the words mean (all Sanskrit):

  • Ekadashi: "the eleventh."
  • Dvadashi: "the twelfth" (from dvā, "two," and daśa, "ten," compare Latin duodecim).
  • Sallekhana: "thinning out," the gradual letting go of the body.
  • Bhishma: "the terrible one," for his fearsome vow.
  • Purva Bhadrapada: "the former lucky feet."

Today's readings through a Sikh lens: passing on the light.

  • The succession of Gurus: Sikhs teach that the same divine light passed from Guru Nanak to each of the nine Gurus who followed, "as one lamp lights another." Before he died, Guru Nanak chose his disciple Lehna, renaming him Angad, "my own limb," over his own sons.

The Guru warns that pride keeps the soul from a Beloved who is already within. Moses' song was meant to call the people back to the God who never left.

The time. Deuteronomy is set on the plains of Moab around the 13th century BC; most scholars date its core to the 7th century BC, under King Josiah, with additions during the exile. Titus was written in Paul's name, probably in the late 1st or early 2nd century AD.

What scholars broadly agree on. The Song of Moses (Deuteronomy 32) is considered one of the oldest poems in the Hebrew Bible, possibly older than the book around it. Deuteronomy's farewell speeches follow the form of ancient Near Eastern treaties, with witnesses, blessings and curses. Crete in the 1st century was a Roman province with a large Jewish community; Titus 1:12 quotes a Cretan poet, usually identified as Epimenides, "Cretans are always liars."

What it meant then. For exiles in Babylon, God "hiding his face" explained the catastrophe, and the song promised a way back. For the churches in Crete, Titus's list of qualities was a guide for choosing trustworthy local leaders.

The allegorical readings. The fathers read Moses' death outside the promised land as a sign that the Law could lead to the border of salvation, but only Jesus (Joshua) could lead people in.

What's debated or unknown. Scholars debate whether Paul wrote the Pastoral Letters (most think not), and when Deuteronomy's Song of Moses was composed. Psalm 90's heading "a prayer of Moses" is ancient tradition, not likely authorship.
