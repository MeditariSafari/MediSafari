November 1st, 2026The five-minute version

The readings: Forty years after crossing the Red Sea, Israel stands at the Jordan, swollen with spring floods. Joshua tells the priests carrying the ark of the covenant to walk into the river. The moment their feet touch the edge of the water, the river upstream stops and piles up in a heap, and the priests stand on dry ground in the middle of the riverbed until the whole nation has crossed. In Matthew, Jesus warns about leaders who "do not practice what they teach," who load heavy burdens on others and love the best seats and titles. Among his followers, he says, "The greatest among you will be your servant." And today, many churches celebrate All Saints: the great cloud of ordinary people whose faith carried them through.

What it means: The river didn't part until the priests got their feet wet. Faith steps in first. The saints we remember today weren't people with titles and honors; they were people who served, stepped into rivers, and carried God's presence into hard places. Jesus' measure of greatness is the same: not who's exalted, but who serves.

Across the traditions: the same truth, in other words.

  • Marcus Aurelius: "Take care that thou art not made into a Caesar, that thou art not dyed with this dye... Keep thyself then simple, good, pure, serious, free from affectation." (Meditations 6.30, Long.)
  • Tao Te Ching: The sage puts himself last and finds himself first; rivers and seas are kings of the valleys because they lie lower (Ch. 7, 66, paraphrased).
  • Sikh: Guru Nanak said sweetness and humility are the essence of all virtues (Asa di Var, paraphrased).
  • Islam: The Prophet said the leader of a people is their servant (paraphrased, from hadith collections).
  • Hindu: The Gita describes the wise as free of pride and arrogance, serving the good of all beings (12:13–15, paraphrased).

Carry it today: Where do you need to step into the water before you see it part?

The theme

  • Feet in the river: The water parts only after the priests step in.
  • Dry ground: The priests stand in the middle until everyone is across.
  • Practice what you teach: Jesus warns against leaders who don't.
  • Servant: Greatness in God's kingdom is measured by service.

Over the past weeks the RCL followed Moses to his death and Joshua to the river. Today Israel crosses into the land, on All Saints' Day, when the church remembers all who have crossed over before us.

1. This Day I Will Exalt You

"This day I will begin to exalt you in the sight of all Israel," God tells Joshua, "so that they may know that I will be with you as I was with Moses."

  • Exalted by God: Joshua doesn't exalt himself. God does, at the moment Joshua leads in obedience.
  • Like Moses: The crossing confirms that the same God who led Moses is leading Joshua.

2. The Waters Cut Off

"The waters of the Jordan flowing from above shall be cut off; they shall stand in a single heap."

  • Flood Season: The text says the Jordan was overflowing its banks at harvest time. This was no shallow stream.
  • Red Sea Echo: The crossing deliberately mirrors the Exodus. The God of the beginning is the God of the ending.

3. Feet in the Water

"The feet of the priests bearing the ark were dipped in the edge of the water," and the water stopped. "The priests who bore the ark of the covenant of the Lord stood firmly on dry ground."

  • Step First: The miracle waits for the first step. Faith moves before the way is clear.
  • Standing in the Middle: The priests stay in the riverbed until the last person crosses. Leaders serve by staying put.

4. Practice What You Teach

"They do not practice what they teach." "They tie up heavy burdens, hard to bear, and lay them on the shoulders of others." "They do all their deeds to be seen by others."

  • Moses' Seat: Jesus respects their teaching; what he faults is the gap between words and life.
  • Burdens: Jesus came to lift burdens: "my yoke is easy, and my burden is light" (Matthew 11:30).

5. The Greatest Will Be Your Servant

"The greatest among you will be your servant. All who exalt themselves will be humbled, and all who humble themselves will be exalted." Joshua is exalted not by seeking it but by obeying. The priests who carry the ark stand in the riverbed, the riskiest place, until everyone else is safe. Paul worked night and day so as not to burden the Thessalonians. On All Saints' Day, the church remembers people like these: not the powerful, but those who served, often unnoticed. The Catholic Gospel today, the Beatitudes, says the same thing another way: blessed are the poor in spirit, the meek, the merciful, the pure of heart. The saints are the ones who stepped into the water first.

For Christians today

All Saints' Day and the crossing of the Jordan have both shaped Christian imagination deeply.

  • The oldest readings: The fathers read the crossing of the Jordan as baptism, and Joshua ("Jesus" in Greek) leading the people into the promised land as Christ leading believers into the kingdom. Origen preached that every Christian must cross their own Jordan.
  • Catholic: All Saints is a Solemnity and a holy day of obligation. The Church teaches the "communion of saints": the living and the dead united in Christ. Tomorrow, All Souls' Day, Catholics pray for the faithful departed.
  • Protestant and evangelical: Many Protestant churches read the names of members who died this past year on All Saints' Sunday. Spirituals like "Deep River" ("I want to cross over into campground") used the Jordan crossing as an image of freedom and of heaven.
  • Orthodox: The Orthodox celebrate All Saints on the Sunday after Pentecost, not November 1. Today they honor Cosmas and Damian, physicians who healed without charge: saints of service.
  • Live debates today: Protestants and Catholics differ on asking saints to pray for us, but agree that the saints are a "great cloud of witnesses" (Hebrews 12:1).

Carry it today: Name one ordinary saint who shaped your faith, and thank God for them.

StoicismWiki ↗

What is Stoicism? A philosophy founded by Zeno of Citium around 300 BC in Athens, named for the painted porch (stoa) where he taught. Its most famous teachers are Epictetus, born into slavery; Seneca, a Roman statesman; and the emperor Marcus Aurelius. The Stoics taught that we can't control events, only our judgments and choices. Their four virtues are wisdom, courage, justice and self-control.

"Take care that thou art not made into a Caesar, that thou art not dyed with this dye; for such things happen. Keep thyself then simple, good, pure, serious, free from affectation, a friend of justice, a worshipper of the gods, kind, affectionate, strenuous in all proper acts." (Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 6.30, Long translation.) The emperor warns himself not to be "dyed" in imperial purple, not to let power change who he is. It's Jesus' warning about seats of honor and titles, from a man who had them all. Keep simple, kind and just; that's the kind of greatness that lasts.

The time. Joshua is set in the late 13th century BC; Matthew wrote around AD 80–90; 1 Thessalonians around AD 50. All Saints' Day was moved to November 1 by Pope Gregory III in the 8th century.

What scholars broadly agree on. Joshua 3–4 combines several sources and is shaped to mirror the Exodus. There are historical reports of the Jordan being blocked by landslides near Adam (named in Joshua 3:16), including in 1267 and 1927. Matthew 23 reflects tensions between Matthew's community and synagogue leaders after AD 70.

What it meant then. For Israel, the crossing was the moment they became a people in their own land. For Matthew's community, Jesus' teaching on servant leadership was a rule for their own leaders.

The allegorical readings. The fathers saw the ark leading the way into the river as Christ going before us into death and opening a path through it.

What's debated or unknown. How much history lies behind the crossing is debated. The location of the crossing near Gilgal and Adam is known approximately but not exactly.
