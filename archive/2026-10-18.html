October 18th, 2026The five-minute version

The readings: Jesus takes his friends to Caesarea Philippi, a city full of shrines to Pan and to Caesar, and asks, "Who do people say that I am?" They report the rumors: John the Baptist, Elijah, Jeremiah. Then he asks, "But who do you say that I am?" Peter blurts out, "You are the Messiah, the Son of the living God." Jesus calls him blessed, says God revealed it to him, and gives him a new name, Peter, "the rock," on which he'll build his church. Paul, writing to Christians who felt spiritually safe, warns them: Israel also had God's gifts in the desert and still fell. "If you think you are standing, watch out that you do not fall." But God is faithful and always provides a way through. And the old teacher Ben Sira says: don't cheat the poor, don't make them wait, listen to them, defend them, and you'll be like a child of God.

What it means: Faith starts with a personal answer. It isn't enough to know what others say about Jesus; at some point each of us is asked, "Who do you say I am?" Today's collect prays that we "persevere with steadfast faith in the confession of your Name," so the answer has to hold up over time. Paul says it can't rest on feeling secure; it has to stand up to testing. And Ben Sira shows what it looks like on a Tuesday afternoon: patience with the poor, a fair word for the oppressed. The rock Jesus builds on is a confession lived out.

Across the traditions: the same truth, in other words.

  • Buddha: "As a solid rock is not shaken by the wind, wise people falter not amidst blame and praise."
  • Confucius: "To see what is right and not to do it is want of courage."
  • Epictetus: if we truly believed "that we are all sprung from God," we would "never have any ignoble or mean thoughts" about ourselves.
  • Bhagavad Gita: Overwhelmed by Krishna's true form, Arjuna asks him, "Tell me who you are" (Ch. 11, paraphrased).
  • Islam: the Qur'an tells the believer, "do not oppress the orphan, and do not repel the one who asks" (93:9–10, paraphrased).

Carry it today: If someone asked you today, "Who do you say Jesus is?", what would your life answer?

The theme

  • The personal question: Jesus moves from "Who do people say?" to "But who do you say?" Faith can't be secondhand.
  • The rock: Peter's confession becomes the foundation, and Peter, impulsive and soon to deny Jesus, becomes "the rock."
  • Watch your footing: Paul warns the confident: "If you think you are standing, watch out that you do not fall." God is faithful and provides a way through.
  • Faith with hands: Ben Sira's care for the poor shows what confessing God looks like in daily life.

Yesterday's RCL showed Moses sheltered in "a cleft of the rock." Today Paul says the rock that followed Israel "was Christ," and Jesus calls Peter the rock. The Collect asks for one thing: that we keep making that confession, steadily, all our lives.

1. Who Do People Say?

"Who do people say that the Son of Man is?"

  • Caesar's City: Caesarea Philippi was named for Caesar. Its cliff held a shrine to Pan and a temple to Augustus, a "son of the divine." Here, of all places, Jesus asks who he is.
  • Good Answers, Not Enough: The crowds' answers, John, Elijah, Jeremiah, are respectful. They are prophets. But they're secondhand.

2. But Who Do You Say?

"He said to them, 'But who do you say that I am?' Simon Peter answered, 'You are the Messiah, the Son of the living God.'"

  • Revealed, Not Figured Out: "Flesh and blood has not revealed this to you, but my Father in heaven." Faith is a gift before it's an achievement.
  • The Living God: In a city of carved gods and a dead emperor called divine, Peter names "the living God."

3. On This Rock

"And I tell you, you are Peter, and on this rock I will build my church, and the gates of Hades will not prevail against it."

  • A Pun: In Greek, Petros (Peter) and petra (rock); in Aramaic, both are Kepha. Simon gets a new name and a new purpose.
  • The Gates of Hades: At Caesarea Philippi, the grotto of Pan was thought by some to be a gateway to the underworld. Jesus says death itself won't win against his church.

4. If You Think You Are Standing

"So if you think you are standing, watch out that you do not fall. No testing has overtaken you that is not common to everyone. God is faithful, and he will not let you be tested beyond your strength, but with the testing he will also provide the way out so that you may be able to endure it."

  • Overconfidence: The Corinthians thought baptism made them safe to flirt with idols. Peter himself, right after his great confession, tries to talk Jesus out of the cross (16:22–23).
  • A Way Through: Paul's comfort is not that testing won't come, but that God stays faithful in it. The collect's word for this is "persevere."

5. Be Like a Child of the Most High

"My child, do not cheat the poor of their living." "Give a hearing to the poor, and return their greeting politely. Rescue the oppressed from the oppressor." "Be a father to orphans, and be like a husband to their mother; you will then be like a son of the Most High, and he will love you more than does your mother." Peter confesses Jesus as "the Son of the living God"; Ben Sira says that whoever cares for the orphan becomes "like a son of the Most High." Confession and compassion are two sides of the same rock. A faith that names Jesus Messiah but cheats the poor has built on sand. The psalms close the morning by calling everything that breathes to praise God; Ben Sira reminds us that God's praise also sounds like a fair wage, a patient hearing and a defended widow. Who do we say Jesus is? We answer with our words on Sunday and with our hands all week.

For Christians today

Matthew 16 is one of the most debated passages in Christian history, and Sirach is read differently across churches.

  • The oldest readings: The church fathers gave several answers to "what is the rock?" Some said Peter himself; many, including Origen and later Augustine, said the rock was Peter's confession of faith, or Christ himself, echoing Paul's "the rock was Christ." All agreed every believer who confesses Christ shares in Peter's role.
  • Catholic: The Catholic Church reads the rock as Peter, the first among the apostles, and his successors the popes. The Catechism teaches that Jesus "entrusted a specific authority to Peter" with the "power of the keys" (§552–553, §881).
  • Protestant and evangelical: Luther and Calvin taught that the rock is Peter's confession of faith in Christ, on which every true church is built, and rejected the papacy's claim to it. Many evangelicals stress the question itself: everyone must answer "Who do you say that I am?" personally.
  • Orthodox: Orthodox theology generally reads the rock as Peter's confession of faith, shared by every bishop who keeps the true faith, and honors Peter as first among equals.
  • Anglican and Episcopal: The Episcopal Church honors Peter's confession with its own feast, the Confession of St. Peter (January 18), which begins the Week of Prayer for Christian Unity. It reads the Apocrypha, like Sirach, in worship "for example of life," but not to establish doctrine.
  • Live debates today: Christians still debate the role of the pope and how to share Peter's ministry among divided churches; Pope John Paul II even invited other churches to help rethink it (Ut Unum Sint, 1995). Another live question, sharpened by Ben Sira: can churches confess Christ faithfully while ignoring the poor?

Carry it today: Answer Jesus' question out loud, in your own words, then do one thing for someone who has less than you.

Today's readings through a Sikh lens: the Guru's word and care for the needy.

Like Paul's promise, a refuge in testing.

StoicismWiki ↗

What is Stoicism? A philosophy founded by Zeno of Citium around 300 BC in Athens, named for the painted porch (stoa) where he taught. Its most famous teachers are Epictetus, born into slavery; Seneca, a Roman statesman; and the emperor Marcus Aurelius. The Stoics taught that we can't control events, only our judgments and choices. Their four virtues are wisdom, courage, justice and self-control.

"If a man should be able to assent to this doctrine as he ought, that we are all sprung from God in an especial manner, and that God is the father both of men and of gods, I suppose that he would never have any ignoble or mean thoughts about himself." (Epictetus, Discourses 1.3, Long translation.) Epictetus, a former slave, taught that every person shares in the divine. Ben Sira says whoever defends the orphan becomes "like a son of the Most High." If the poor person in front of you is also a child of God, you can't cheat them, and you can't think meanly of yourself either.

Today's readings through Hindu and Jain eyes: Maha Ashtami and "who are you?"

  • The Hindu lens: Today is Maha Ashtami, the eighth and greatest day of Navaratri, honoring Mahagauri, the radiant white form of the Goddess. Many families honor young girls as living images of the Goddess (Kanya Puja), feeding and blessing them, and the Sandhi Puja is performed at the moment Ashtami turns to Navami. In the Bhagavad Gita, Arjuna sees Krishna's universal form and asks, trembling, who he truly is (ch. 11, paraphrased), the same question Jesus puts to Peter from the other side.
  • The Jain lens: Jains teach that every soul (jiva) is divine by nature, and that compassion for every living being is the first duty. Jain communities are known for animal shelters, hospitals and food for the poor.

Today is Shukla Ashtami, the eighth day of the bright half (Saptami ends at 8:28 AM India time), with the moon moving from Purva Ashadha into Uttara Ashadha (per Indian calendars; exact Chicago times couldn't be confirmed). Tomorrow is Maha Navami.

What the words mean (all Sanskrit):

  • Mahagauri: mahā, "great," plus gaurī, "white, radiant."
  • Ashtami: "the eighth" (from aṣṭa, "eight," compare Latin octo).
  • Kanya: "girl, maiden."
  • Sandhi: "joining," the meeting point of two days.
  • Uttara Ashadha: "the latter invincible one."

The time. Ben Sira wrote around 180 BC in Jerusalem, under Greek rule; his grandson translated the book into Greek after 132 BC. Paul wrote to Corinth around AD 53–55. Peter's confession took place around AD 29, not long before Jesus' final journey to Jerusalem.

What scholars broadly agree on. Caesarea Philippi, at the foot of Mount Hermon near one of the sources of the Jordan, was known as Paneas for its shrine to Pan. Herod the Great built a temple to Augustus there around 20 BC, and his son Philip rebuilt the city and named it for Caesar. The springs flowed from a deep cave that ancient writers associated with the underworld. Peter clearly held a leading role among the first disciples; Paul calls him "Cephas," the rock, and treats him as a pillar of the Jerusalem church.

What it meant then. "Messiah" (Greek Christos) means "anointed one," the king Israel hoped God would send. "Son of the living God" set Jesus against Caesar's claims to divinity. For Paul's Corinthians, the warning about idol feasts was practical: most meat in the market had passed through a pagan temple.

The allegorical readings. The fathers read the rock that followed Israel as Christ, giving water in the desert, a figure of baptism and the Eucharist. Many medieval preachers read Peter's keys as the power to forgive sins given to the whole church.

What's debated or unknown. Scholars debate whether Jesus' words to Peter (16:17–19) go back to Jesus or reflect Matthew's church, since Mark and Luke don't include them. The meaning of "the rock" divides churches to this day. Sirach's original Hebrew was lost for centuries; large parts were rediscovered in a Cairo synagogue storeroom in 1896 and at Masada.
